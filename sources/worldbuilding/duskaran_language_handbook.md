# Introduction

Duskaran emerged over some eight hundred Ancestor Cycles from the linguistic necessity of survival. The *Stellar Horizon* carried two peoples. The settlers, some thousands of them in cryogenic stasis, were African. Most came from East Africa: they belonged to many peoples and already used Swahili as their common language. A minority came from West Africa and spoke Hausa. The crew, a few hundred, was Asian: Chinese officers and engineers who spoke Mandarin, and Filipino ratings who spoke Tagalog. English was the working language between crew and settlers, and between people and the ship systems. Nobody spoke it at home.

Three facts explain the language that followed.

- **Swahili was already a common language.** After landfall it was the speech of almost everybody, and it supplied about seven words in ten.
- **The others learned it as adults.** Hausa speakers, the crew and their families dropped the noun classes and the verb inflection and kept the words. The same thing happened on Earth in the reduced Swahili of traders and newcomers. Duskaran grammar is the result: verbs that do not change, particles for tense, and old prefixes frozen on the nouns (*matunda*, *mwalimu*, *kuja*).
- **English had one function, and it ended.** When the ship stopped working, English had no more use. It left the words that were tied to the work.

Hausa, Mandarin and Tagalog survive in special fields and in names. Duskarans call their language Creole. A linguist of Earth would call it a restructured Swahili.

The language reflects its speakers' priorities: water, wind, survival, and community. Its phonology accommodates communication during storms, its vocabulary encodes practical knowledge, and its structure allows rapid information exchange in life-threatening situations.

# Phonology

## Vowels

Duskaran uses five cardinal vowels, pronounced clearly to cut through wind noise:

- **a** /a/ – as in "father"
- **e** /e/ – as in "bay"
- **i** /i/ – as in "see"
- **o** /o/ – as in "low"
- **u** /u/ – as in "boot"

**Long vowels.** A doubled vowel is long, and length separates words: *taa* /taː/ "light" and *ta* /ta/ "under"; *kaa* /kaː/ "stay" and *ka* /ka/ "the"; *kuu* /kuː/ "great". A long vowel is held for about twice the time of a short one, which also carries well in wind.

Diphthongs are simplified or avoided to maintain clarity during storms. The sequences *ai* and *au* survive, mostly in loanwords (*taiyo*, *saitaru*, *shauri*).

## Consonants

| Type | Sounds |
|------|--------|
| **Stops** | p, b, t, d, k, g |
| **Affricates** | ch /tʃ/, j /dʒ/ |
| **Fricatives** | f, v, s, z, sh /ʃ/, h |
| **Aspirates** | th /tʰ/, dh /dʰ/ (rare; Swahili-derived words only) |
| **Nasals** | m, n, ny /nj/, ng or ŋ /ŋ/ |
| **Liquids** | l, r |
| **Glides** | w, y /j/ |

**Spelling:**

- **ng** and **ŋ** are the same sound, /ŋ/: *hanga* /ˈha.ŋa/, *daŋa* /ˈda.ŋa/. Each word keeps its established spelling. The sequence /ŋg/ is written **ŋg**: *luŋga* /ˈluŋ.ga/.
- **j** is always /dʒ/ (*maji* /ˈma.dʒi/). **y** is the glide /j/ (*kiya* /ˈki.ja/).

**Key Features:**

- The velar nasal /ŋ/ is common, reinforced by Mandarin and Tagalog
- Aspiration is phonemic but subtle, primarily in emphasis contexts
- No phonemic tone, but pitch variations convey emotion and urgency

## Phonotactics

- **Syllable Structure**: (C)V(C) in the core vocabulary, simple and easily shouted. Swahili-derived words add prenasalized onsets (*mb*, *nd*, *nj*, *nz*, *mv*) and a syllabic *m* before a consonant (*mto*, *mji*).
- **Clusters**: Otherwise limited to initial position; typically nasal + stop, or stop or fricative + liquid or glide. Earth loanwords keep a few more (*sistamu*, *kriyo*).
- **Stress**: Generally falls on the penultimate syllable. In hyphenated compounds the first element keeps the main stress (*maji-tamu* /ˈma.dʒi.ta.mu/).
- **Vowel Hiatus**: Allowed between different vowels (*ua*, *toa*); a glide may be heard in fast speech
- **Hyphen and apostrophe**: A hyphen joins an ordinary compound; each part keeps its meaning (*maji-tamu*, "sweet water"; *maji-tunga*, "water finder", the person). An apostrophe marks a **fused** compound: it is spoken as one word with one stress, and it names one thing that is more than its parts. Psychic abilities and ritual terms are fused (*maji'tunga*, "water finding", the ability; *joto'hisi*; *hangan'kiya*; *maji'bara*; *baŋga'sora*). Very old compounds are written as one word (*hangakora*, *korasha*, *kinabara*)

# Grammar Overview

## Word Order

Subject-Verb-Object (SVO), with flexibility for emphasis.

## Morphology

- **Agglutinative tendencies**: Affixes build meaning without heavy inflection
- **Minimal conjugation**: Context and particles indicate tense, not verb forms
- **Derivational suffixes**: Productive word-building from roots

## Key Grammatical Elements

- **Pronouns**: *ni* (I), *mi* (we), *zi* (you), *ŋa* (they). None carries gender, and *zi* and *ŋa* serve for both singular and plural
- **Pluralization**: Suffix **-ya** or context-dependent
- **Possession**: Possessor follows possessed (*kibanda mi*, "our shelter"; *roho ni*, "my spirit")
- **Modifiers**: Adjectives and numerals follow the noun (*hanga manga*, "great wind"; *maji tano*, "five measures of water")
- **Article**: Optional definite article **ka** before the noun; indefinite nouns take no article
- **Copula**: **ja** (is/are); before a verb it marks a habitual action
- **Negation**: Particle **ma** before the verb or copula
- **Questions**: Rising intonation, the final particle **ke**, or a question word left in place (*nini* "what", *gani* "which", *wapi* "where", *lini* "when")
- **Tense markers**: **kora** (past), **tuka** (future), placed before the verb; the present is unmarked
- **Aspect markers**: **bado** (still), **tayari** (already)
- **Prepositions**: **na** (at, in, on), **kwa** (to, for), **toka** (from), **ta** (under), **su** (with; it also joins nouns as "and")
- **Derivation**: **-ka** makes a verb from a noun (*shauri* → *shaurika*, "to consult"); **ta-** makes a noun from a verb (*shanda* → *tashanda*, "a trade"); **-ji** names a person, **-nga** an agent, **-wan** a place
- **And, or, but**: **su** joins nouns, verbs and clauses; **au** is "or"; **lakini** is "but"
- **Verbs never change form.** Inflected Swahili forms survive only inside a few fixed expressions (*haki-ya-wote*)
- **Frozen prefixes**: Swahili prefixes survive as part of the word and carry no grammar. *ma-* does not mark a plural (*matunda*, "fruit", one or many), *ku-* does not mark an infinitive (*kuja*, "come"), and *m-*, *ki-*, *u-* do not mark a class

## Sentence Patterns

**Commands.** The bare verb is a command: *Kuja!* ("Come!"). *Tafadhali* makes a request: *Tafadhali kaa.* ("Please stay."). A negative command takes *ma*: *Ma go!* ("Do not go!").

**Wishes.** *Heri* at the start of the sentence marks a wish or a blessing: *Heri hanga zi ja sawa.* ("May your wind be balanced.").

**Being, having, existing.** *Ja* links a noun to a noun, a quality or a place: *Maji ja safi.* ("The water is clean."), *Maji ja hapa.* ("There is water here."). "Have" is *ja su*, "be with": *Ni ja su maji.* ("I have water."); *Mi ma ja su wakati.* ("We have no time.").

**Conditions and reasons.** *Kama* is "if" and *sababu* is "because": *Kama tufani kuja, mi kaa.* ("If the storm comes, we stay."); *Mi kaa sababu tufani kuja.* ("We stay because the storm is coming.").

**Relative clauses.** A short clause follows its noun with no marker: *hanga pita*, "the wind that passes". A longer clause takes *amba*: *jata amba kora leta maji*, "the person who brought the water".

**Comparison.** *Zaidi* is "more" and *kuliko* is "than": *Bonde ja baridi zaidi kuliko mji.* ("The valley is colder than the city."). "Most" is *zaidi kuliko wote*, "more than all", or *zaidi* alone when the context is clear.

**Self and each other.** *Mwenyewe* is "self": *Ni fanya mwenyewe.* ("I do it myself."). There is no passive: an unknown agent is *ŋa* or *jata* (*Ŋa kora vunja bomba*, "The pipe was broken", lit. "they broke the pipe").

**Numbers above ten.** Tens are a number before *kumi*: *mbili-kumi* (20), *tatu-kumi* (30), *tisa-kumi* (90). The old word *ishirini* (20) survives in formal counting. Units follow with *su*: *kumi su moja* (11), *tatu-kumi su tano* (35). *Mia* and *elfu* take the number after them, like any noun: *mia mbili* (200), *elfu tatu* (3,000).

## Storm Register

Short words that differ by one sound are a risk in high wind. In a storm, on a caravan line or over a signal horn, speakers use a fixed set of longer forms. Every child learns them with the hand signals.

| Meaning | Normal | Storm register |
|---------|--------|----------------|
| not, do not | *ma* | *hapana* at the start: *Hapana go!* |
| I | *ni* | *ni peke* ("I alone") |
| we | *mi* | *mi wote* ("we all") |
| yes | *ndiyo* | *ndiyo* twice |
| a number | *tano* | said twice: *tano, tano* |

The key word comes first and is repeated: *Tufani! Tufani kuja!*

# Core Vocabulary

Duskaran has two layers of vocabulary. The **core** layer formed in the first generations after landfall: short words, often with /ŋ/, for the things people named every day. The larger **Swahili-derived** layer supplied most of the remaining vocabulary. Where both layers have a word for the same thing, the core word is the everyday form and the Swahili-derived word is more formal or technical. Such pairs are marked *core* and *formal* below.

# Compound Formation Patterns

Duskaran creates new vocabulary through systematic compounding:

## Type 1: Noun + Noun

- **hanga-kuta** = wind + wall → windbreak
- **maji-bomba** = water + pipe → aqueduct
- **joto-kanda** = warm + face → day hemisphere

## Type 2: Noun + Verb-Derived

- **hangan'kiya** = wind + craft → weatherworking
- **maji'tunga** = water + sense → water finding

## Type 3: Noun + Adjective

- **manta-tana** = tree + strong → ancient stormroot
- **hanga-kali** = wind + fierce → gale

## Type 4: Verb + Agent Suffix

- **shanda-ji** = trade + person → trader
- **tula-nga** = build + agent → builder

## Type 5: Descriptive Compounds

- **jua-ŋoga** = sun + reach → sunspire
- **kivuli-dansa** = shadow + dance → shadow-dance

# Regional Dialect Variations

All dialects are understood everywhere on the Living Edge. They differ in speed, in a few sounds, and in which of two words a speaker prefers.

## Heartland Standard

The speech of Birni and the cities around the Bahari, and the form that this handbook describes.

- Clipped, efficient speech, with rapid delivery adapted to storm interruptions
- Wind-related metaphors are common
- Other regions say that a heartlander "speaks like the central wind"

## Northern Dialect (Tanga-Barafu)

The north keeps the most Mandarin words, because the crew families settled the fortified cities there.

- *biŋ* for ice in daily speech, where the heartland says *barafu*
- *shan* for any height, where the heartland says *mlima*
- *cheŋ* for a city, where the heartland says *mji*
- Final /ŋ/ is strong, and vowels before it are short
- Locative forms in *-ni* are common: *barafuni*, "at the ice"

## Harmatan and Caravan Dialect

The speech of Harmatan and of the caravan roads keeps the most Hausa words.

- *iska* for wind in daily speech, where the heartland says *hanga* (*hanga* stays in ritual and in weatherworking)
- *zaŋgo* for any stopping place; *kofa* for a gate or a pass
- *sanyi* is used for all cold, and *baridi* sounds formal
- Greetings are long: a speaker asks about the family, the water and the road before any business

## Deepkin Dialect

- Slower and more melodic, shaped by the echo of stone chambers
- Long vowels are held longer than on the surface
- A large vocabulary for warmth, darkness and kinds of light: *taa-kivuli* ("shadow-light") for bioluminescence, and *liwanag*, poetic on the surface, as the daily word for a strong light
- The evidential *tunga* is frequent, because much is sensed and little is seen
- Surface speakers call it the "night-tongue"

## Dayward Edge Dialect

- Abbreviated forms: *joto-kali* shortens to *jokali* ("burning hot")
- *init* for the heat of the day side, as an intensity word: *init sana*, "deadly hot"
- The storm register is used in normal speech, because the wind there seldom drops

# Trade Pidgin (Shanda-Speak)

A reduced register for commerce between settlements. It is used with the hand signals of trade, and a deal can be made with almost no speech.

## Key Features

- No article, no copula, no tense marker except *tuka*
- Noun first, number after, and numbers are said twice when the amount matters
- A fixed set of about two hundred words
- *Nzuri* closes a deal and *ma nzuri* refuses it

## Examples

- **Zi shanda?** – You trade?
- **Maji tano.** – Water, five (units)
- **Bei nini?** – What price?
- **Nzuri kabisa.** – Very good. (Deal)
- **Ma nzuri.** – Not good. (No deal)
- **Tuka kuja.** – Will come later.

## Everyday Dialogues

### Morning Greeting at Settlement

**A:** Hujambo, muru. Wara zi wote ja nzuri ke?
"Hello, friend. Is your whole family well?"

**B:** Ndiyo, asante sana. Kazi na moto-bomba tayari isha. Zi tuka kuja kwa hanga-sikia na sori ke?
"Yes, thank you very much. The work on the thermal exchanger is already finished. Will you come to the wind-listening at *sori*?"

**A:** Su furaha. Pumua pamoja su jamii leta amani kwa ni.
"With joy. Breathing together with the community brings me peace."

### Council Discussion

**Elder:** Kora kura, jata gani taka sema?
"Before the vote, who wants to speak?"

**Young Adult:** Ni omba ruhusa. Ni pendekeza mi badili zamuya kazi, ili jata ma beba mzigo kubwa peke.
"I ask permission. I propose that we change the work rotations, so that no one carries a big burden alone."

**Elder:** Nenoya zi nala babuya mi su ahadiya ushirika. Asante.
"Your words honor our ancestors and the promises of cooperation. Thank you."

### Children's Education

**Teacher:** Ni uliza: sifa gani ja muhimu zaidi kwa ishi na Duskara?
"I ask: which quality is most important for living on Duskara?"

**Child:** Uvumilivu, su ujasiri kwa omba msaada.
"Endurance, and the courage to ask for help."

**Teacher:** Sawa kabisa. Kumbuka: mi kutana hanga-kali pamoja.
"Exactly right. Remember: we meet the fierce wind together."

### Feast Preparation

**Elder:** Karamu-pindi tuka kuja haraka. Wara zi tayari fanya kikombeya shukrani ke?
"The Gradient Feast comes soon. Has your family already made gratitude cups?"

**Parent:** Ndiyo. Mi kora fanya kikombeya mpya kwa baŋaya, ili ŋa kumbuka sehemu ŋa na jamii.
"Yes. We made new ones for the children, so that they remember their part in the community."

**Elder:** Baraka nzuri. Heri hanga zi bado ja sawa.
"A beautiful blessing. May your wind stay balanced."

### Partnership Negotiation

**Person A:** Ni taka sema kwa ushirika mi. Ni sema wazi ke?
"I want to talk about our partnership. May I speak openly?"

**Person B:** Ndiyo, tafadhali. Uwazi ja heshima kubwa.
"Yes, please. Openness is great respect."

**Person A:** Ni hisi mzigo nyumba ma ja sawa. Mi shanda mpya ke?
"I feel the household burden is not balanced. Can we negotiate again?"

**Person B:** Asante kwa ujasiri zi. Mi tuka sema su fanya pamoja.
"Thank you for your courage. We will talk and work it out together."

# Etymology Notes

The shares below are estimates for the whole vocabulary.

## Swahili (about 70%)

The common language of the East African settlers, and the base of the Creole:

- Most nouns, verbs and descriptive words
- Numbers
- Social and ethical concepts (*jamii*, *heshima*, *utu*)

## English (about 10%)

The working language between crew and settlers, and of the ship systems. Nobody spoke it at home, and it left few words, but they are frequent:

- Short working words (*go*, *kin*, *dok*)
- Technical terms (*tekka*, *sistamu*, *kriyo*, *saitaru*, *kompyu*)

## Hausa (about 8%)

The language of the West African minority among the settlers:

- Words of the caravan road and the city (*birni*, *zaŋgo*, *kofa*, *rijiya*, *hamada*)
- Wind and weather words in place names (*iska*, *sanyi*, *harmatan*)
- Personal names and line names; the prefix *Dan-* of place lines

## Mandarin (about 6%)

The language of the officers and engineers:

- Short place-name words (*cheŋ*, *shan*, *feŋ*, *biŋ*, *loŋ*)
- *taiyo*, "star" (from *tàiyáng*, "sun")
- Personal names and line names
- The high frequency of /ŋ/

## Tagalog (about 6%)

The language of the Filipino ratings:

- Landscape words in place names (*bundok*, *lambak*, *patag*, *bukal*, *lawa*)
- *liwanag*, kept in poetic use beside everyday *taa*
- Personal names and line names

## Evolved Innovations (Post-Landing)

Compounds and terms unique to Duskara:

- **hangan'kiya** (weatherworking)
- **hanga-daŋa** (twilight edge)
- **joto-kanda** (day face)
- The core layer of short everyday words (*baŋa*, *luŋga*, *manga*, *ŋolu*, *nala*)
- All psychic ability terminology

# Naming Conventions

The Duskaran Names Guidelines give the full system. In short:

**Personal name + kin-\[Wind-Kin\] + line name** (full formal): *Amira kin-Hanga Wekesa*  
**Personal name + line name** (common formal): *Amira Wekesa*  
**Personal name** (casual): *Amira*

- **Personal names** come from three stocks: settler names (Swahili, Hausa: *Jabari*, *Tanko*), crew names (Mandarin, Tagalog: *Meilin*, *Tala*), and Duskaran names made of Creole words (*Tufani*, *Hangapole*). Names are not marked for gender.
- **Line names** (*ukoo*) are ship lines from Earth (*Kamau*, *Chen*, *Bello*, *Dalisay*), craft lines (*Majitunga*, *Tulanga*), or place lines with *Dan-*, "child of" (*Dan-Bahari*).
- **Wind-Kin**: *kin-Hanga* (Wind), *kin-Moto* (Fire), *kin-Maji* (Water), *kin-Babu* (Elder lineage), *kin-Kivuli* (Shadow, Deepkin).

# Advanced Grammar Notes

## Aspect Markers (Beyond Basic Tense)

- **bado** /ˈba.do/ – still, continuing (from Swahili)
- **tayari** /ta.ˈja.ri/ – already, completed (from Swahili)
- **mara kwa mara** /ˈma.ra.kwa.ma.ra/ – repeatedly (from Swahili)

**Examples:**

- *Mi bado shanda.* – We still trade. (ongoing)
- *Zi tayari kula.* – You already ate. (completed)

## Evidentiality (How Speaker Knows)

A speaker can say how the information was obtained. The evidential word stands alone at the start of the sentence, followed by a short pause. It is a full word, not a prefix, and the sentence after it is unchanged.

- **ona** /ˈo.na/ – seen directly
- **sikia** /si.ˈki.a/ – heard from another person
- **tunga** /ˈtu.ŋa/ – sensed psychically

**Examples:**

- *Ona, tufani kuja.* – (I saw it:) a storm is coming.
- *Sikia, maji isha na Liko-Luŋga.* – (I heard:) the water is running out at Liko-Luŋga.
- *Tunga, hatari ja hapa.* – (I sense it:) danger is here.

Evidentials are expected in reports to a council and in storm warnings. A warning without one is taken as *ona*.

## Honorifics

- **bapa/ama** – respectful address to elders
- **kiya-** prefix – honored weatherworker (e.g., *Kiya-Amira*)
- **babu-** prefix – venerated ancestor/elder
