# Introduction

The Compendium describes how Duskara works. The Gazetteer says where things are. This Daybook says how it is to be there: what a person sees, hears, smells and touches in an ordinary day, what the hands do without thought, and what people feel about it.

It is a tool for fiction. Use it to choose the details of a scene. It adds no history and no geography.

## The Reference Place

Unless a section says otherwise, the Daybook describes a town of the heartland, in the nightward half of the Central band: cool, windy and grey, with enough water. The chapter The Four Lands and the Two Edges gives the differences. The chapter Under the Night gives the caves.

# The Grain of the World

On the surface of Duskara everything has a direction, and the direction does not change.

| What | Comes from | Goes to | Source |
|------|------------|---------|--------|
| Light | The day side | | (C00) |
| Heat | The day side | | (C00) |
| Wind at the ground | The night side | The day side | (G) |
| Water | The ice of the night side | The salt flats of the Dayward Edge | (G) |
| The ground | High under the ice | Low toward the day side | (G) |
| The dead | They live in the wind | | (C06) |

Light and heat come from one side. Wind, water and the ancestors come from the other. People live on the strip where the two cross.

- **Nobody is lost in ordinary weather.** A person who faces the light has the wind at the back. Grain, grass and smoke all lean the same way (N). To be lost is a thing of storms, mist, caves and closed rooms, and it frightens a Duskaran more than it would frighten a person of Earth.
- **Nightward is the hard way.** It is uphill, upwind and upstream, into the cold and the dark. Water comes from there. Dayward is downhill, downwind and downstream: the easy way, and the place where water ends.
- **The word for what is right is *sawa*, balanced** (H). The Central band is *Daŋa-Sawa*, the balanced edge (G). The blessing is *Heri hanga zi ja sawa*, "may your wind be balanced" (H). Too much of the day and too much of the night are equal faults.
- **Directions serve at every size.** Creole has no east or west (G). It uses the four directions of the belt for small things too: the nightward shelf, the dayward stair, the nightward tower (N), the up-belt end of a table. Left and right are for the two hands and for nothing else.

## Figures of Speech

A stock for dialogue and for close narration.

| Saying | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| It went dayward | It is lost or wasted and will not come back. Water that reaches the salt does not return. |
| To go nightward for it | To take the hard way; to go to the source of a matter. |
| Upwind of it | Before it; not yet touched by it. |
| Downwind of them | Living with the results of what other people do. |
| To give someone your lee (*toa kinga*) | To put yourself between a person and what is hard for them. |
| To sweep nightward | To do useless work. |
| Side-on | Not fighting a thing and not giving way to it (N). |

## The Exception of the Rift

In the rift of the heartland the river and the cold current of the floor run along the belt, not across it (G). Heartlanders are the only Duskarans who see water go sideways for a long distance. A person from another region who comes to the Bahari stands and looks at the river for some time.

# Light

## The Sun

- Its daily name is *sora*. The formal name is *simba-taiyo*, the lion-star, and people call it the Lion (H).
- It is a red dwarf (C00). Its disc is about three times as wide as the sun of Earth: one finger at arm's length covers it. This follows from the mass of the star and the distance of the planet (C00).
- Its color is that of an ember, between red and orange.
- **It does not move.** It stands at the dayward horizon, in the same place, for the whole of a life. A person can point to it with closed eyes.
- It gives light. It gives no warmth that the skin can feel, except on the Dayward Edge. A Duskaran is warmed by air, by walls and by the ground, not by the sun.
- Through the haze of the day wall it is dull enough to look at for one breath. Children are told not to.
- It flares (C00). For some minutes a white point stands on the disc and the light turns hard. People then expect an aurora and bad signals within a day or two (C00), and those who have an ability expect a bad head (C13).

How much of the disc stands above the ground tells a person how far dayward they are.

| Where | Offset | On level ground with a clear horizon |
|-------|-------:|--------------------------------------|
| Nightward limit of the belt | -150 | A red glow, and at best the rim |
| Middle of the Nightward Edge | -100 | A low arc, about a quarter of the height of the disc |
| Nightward limit of the Central band | -50 | Half the disc |
| Center line | 0 | Most of the disc, cut at its foot |
| Dayward limit of the Central band | +50 | The whole disc, resting on the ground |
| Middle of the Dayward Edge | +100 | The whole disc, clear of the ground by a third of its width |
| Dayward limit of the belt | +150 | The whole disc, clear of the ground by more than half its width |

The Central band is the country where the ground cuts the sun. These figures are a guide for description, not a survey.

- **Height shows more sun.** A tower, a roof or a scarp lifts the eye, and more of the disc comes into view. A climb of a hundred meters adds about a fifth of the disc. Children go up on roofs to see it.
- **A hill on the dayward side hides it for good.** Some houses, and some whole streets, have no sun and never will. Everybody knows which ones.

## The Sky

- **Dayward.** A band of copper and amber stands above the horizon, widest around the sun. This is the day wall, seen from far off: the dust and the heat that stand beyond the Dayward Edge (G). The sun is seen through it. It has three states. When it is **clear**, the disc is hard and red and every shadow has an edge. When it is **veiled**, which is the usual state in the Central band, the disc is a red stain in the copper and shadows are soft. When it is **shut**, in a dust phase, there is no disc for days: the light turns brown and nothing has a shadow.
- **Overhead.** Grey, with violet in it. The Creole word for grey is *hanga-rangi*, wind-color (H). This is the "same grey light as ever" of the heartland (N).
- **Nightward.** Dark. From open ground a long low bank shows on that horizon and does not move: the night wall, over the ice (G). Its top takes the red of the sun and its foot is black. Lightning walks in it. From the floor of the rift, under the scarp, it is out of sight.
- **Aurora** (*anga-taa*). Green and red curtains above the night wall, after a flare. On the Nightward Edge they are common. In the Central band a strong one is an event, and a child born under it may carry its name (C19).
- **Stars.** A few bright ones show low over the night in clear weather, and many more on the Nightward Edge. They drift through the Cycle, and the Archivists keep the count by them (C10). Most people of the Central band cannot name one.
- **No moon** (C06).
- **Cloud goes two ways.** Low cloud moves dayward with the wind. High cloud, and the dust of the day side, move nightward above it (G). Copper bands in a storm cloud are that dust coming down (C02).

## Light on the Ground

- **Shadows do not move.** When the sun is clear they are long and they point nightward. Every wall has a lit face and a dark face for all time. Moss, damp and frost keep to the dark face. Things dry on the lit face.
- A person who walks nightward walks on their own shadow. A person who walks dayward has the light in the eyes: they pull a scarf or a brim forward, and lines come early at the corners of the eyes.
- **The light does not change with the bell.** It changes only with the weather: a line of storm, a dust phase, mist off the lake.
- **Color.** The low red light makes red, ochre, yellow and white strong. Blue and green look dark. Cloth and paint use white, red, ochre and black, in bold figures: the same taste as the script, which is made of thick strokes of high contrast (C18). The blue-gray robe of a water judge (C04) looks like slate out of doors.
- **People help the light.** Walls that face the day are washed white. Cairns carry reflective tops (G). The rail of the Whisper Road shows as a bright line across the country (N).
- **Birni.** The city rises in terraces from the nightward shore of the Bahari (G), so it faces the sun across 45 kilometers of water. On a clear day a road of red light lies on the lake from the far shore to the foot of the city.

## Light Indoors

- Rooms are dim. Windows are small and high, on the dayward wall.
- A house has three lights, and each has its color. **Glowcap jars** give a soft blue-green (C17). **Oil lamps** give yellow, and the oil is measured (N). **Power** from the wind banks goes first to pumps, growing towers, rail and public works (C09); a house has a small share by quota (C11), enough for a hot plate or one lamp at set bells.
- A face looks different in each: warm and red in the doorway, green beside the jar, yellow over the lamp.
- Darkness is not given on the surface. People make it. See Sleep.

# Wind

## On the Body

- The wind at the ground comes from the night side, always (G). On the floor of the rift it is about 20 kilometers an hour. On open ground it is 30 to 40. The north is the windiest region (G).
- At 20, cloth and hair move, dust walks along the ground, and a hat stays on. At 40, a person leans to walk nightward, speaks loud, and holds on to anything light.
- People who grow up in it lean on it and do not know that they lean (N). When they step into a lee they fall a half-step forward. Country people do this at every city gate, and city children laugh.
- **The stance is side-on:** one shoulder to the wind, feet apart (N). Two equals who stop to talk in the open stand face to face along the belt, each with a shoulder to the night.
- **One cheek is cold.** On a path along the belt the nightward cheek loses its feeling, and the eye on that side runs (N). Ears ache, so the scarf covers them.
- **The back is the cold part of a person who sits,** because every seat faces the day. Coats are thickest across the back, and collars are high.

## Sound

- Out of doors it is never silent. The wind is a hum under every other sound (C03).
- Each place has its own note. Roofs hum under their nets of cable (N). Towers give a low note that a person feels in the back teeth (N). Turbines beat. Storm chimes (C10), wind-flutes (C06), flags and kites add to it. A person who comes home knows the town by ear before they see it.
- Buildings creak and groan when the pressure shifts (C03). People who live in a house know its sounds and wake when one of them is wrong.
- **There are two voices.** The outdoor voice is loud and short, and the hands help it (C18). The indoor voice is low. A person who brings the outdoor voice into a room is told so.
- **Sound goes dayward.** A bell is heard far down the wind and hardly at all up it. The bell house and the drum stand at the nightward end of a town.
- In high wind people use the storm register and say numbers twice (H).

## Lee and Stillness

These are two different things, and people feel them in opposite ways.

- **Lee** (*kinga*, H) is shelter. The wind is still heard overhead, and here it does not reach. A court, a wall, the back of a friend. It is the Duskaran idea of comfort.
- **Stillness** is when the wind itself stops. It is rare. Flags hang, the roofs go quiet, and the animals stop. People stop too, and look nightward. Still air means that cold is collecting on the ground (G), or that something is holding a line. Children are sent indoors.

## What the Wind Does to Habits

- Nothing light is left loose. Every object has a cord, a peg, a net or a stone on it. Children tie good knots early (N).
- Paper and flame are for indoors. Out of doors people use slate, a tally board (N) and knots in a cord.
- Water is poured low, in the lee, with the spout on the rim of the cup.
- Dust is swept out of the dayward door, with the wind.
- Smoke lies flat as soon as it leaves the vent (N) and goes dayward. Fires stand on the dayward side of a court.
- A kite flies with no skill at all. It is a child's first toy. Houses fly a family kite at the Storm Wall Festival (C15).
- Seeds and spores travel on the wind (C16). In some phases the winged seeds of the windvine cross a town like a slow rain, and people sneeze.

# Air

## Smell

**A person smells only what is nightward of them.** The wind does not turn, so it never brings a smell from the day side. A town smells the country nightward of it and does not smell itself.

- The base smell of open air is the smell of the night: ice, wet stone, cold.
- Every town has a **clean end** and a **used end**. At the nightward end are the wall, the belt of stormroot (N), the turbines, the condensers, which face the damp air as it arrives (G), and the cisterns. At the dayward end are the goat pens, the recyclers (C09), the drying yards, the tanners and the forges, and the greenhouses, which need the light (C11).
- People who live at the dayward end live downwind. It is said plainly and without shame, but everybody knows who does.

| Place | Smell |
|-------|-------|
| Open ground, nightward of a town | Cold stone and ice; wet earth after a line |
| The stormroot belt | Resin and damp bark |
| The lanes | Dust, hearth smoke, lamp oil, the sour of fermenting porridge |
| A court | Goat hair, oiled wool, flour, the porridge crock |
| A growing tower | Wet roots, green leaf, the pond smell of the feed water |
| A cistern | Cold, wet stone |
| A river or lake quarter | Mud, fish, wet rope (N) |
| The dayward end | Goats, drying fish, the recyclers, hot metal |
| Near a vent | Sulfur (C03) |
| Before a storm | Copper on the tongue and a sharp smell of charge; sulfur with it before the worst ones (C02) |
| After rain | Wet dust |

- **Wet dust is the best-loved smell on Duskara.** It is the smell of the Water's Blessing Festival (C10). Old people stand in a doorway for it.
- **A smell from the day side is an alarm.** Scorched rock and dry dust mean that hot air has come in against the wind, as it does at the hot gates (G). Everybody looks up.
- **People** smell of smoke, wool and skin oil. A person who has come off the road smells of dust, and one who has come from the steam house smells of resin.

## Taste

- **Water has a home taste.** Melt water is flat and very cold. Cistern water tastes of stone. Condenser water tastes of nothing, and people put a grain of salt or a leaf in it. The springs of the Fire Land taste of the rock. A traveler knows the water of home with the first mouthful.
- "How is your water?" is a real question (N). The proper answer is that it is sweet.
- ***Tamu*, sweet, means good water before it means anything else** (H). The sweetness of sugar is rare: dried fruit, at feasts (C11).
- New water upsets the belly. This is one reason for the boiled cup of welcome (C11).
- The mouth is dry. Lips crack and people oil them. There is grit between the teeth after any walk on open ground.

## Warm and Cold

- The belt runs from -5 degrees at the nightward limit to +40 at the dayward limit (C00). A heartland town in the nightward half has about 12 to 18 degrees, with wind. People wear a coat and a scarf (N) for all of their lives.
- **Temperature belongs to places, not to times.** There is no cold hour and no warm one. A person knows the warm corner of the court, the cold step of the stair, and the turn of the path where the scarf goes on (N).
- So a change in a known place is noticed at once, and it worries people (N).
- Cold lies in hollows and against the dark face of a wall. Warmth stays on lit walls and near the ground of the Fire Land.
- The wind is dry in most phases. Mist stands near the ice, the springs and the lake.

# Water in the Hand

Canon gives the frame. Water is the most precious thing, and waste is the worst fault (C03, C06). A Resource Allocation Committee sets the rations (C11). Water tokens belong to the people who have authority over valves (C04). This chapter gives what happens in a kitchen.

## The Measure

- Each person has a **measure** (*kibaba*, H) of sweet water for one day. In the heartland it is about ten liters: one jar, which a child of a hundred Cycles can carry. It is smaller in the Dry Land and larger in the Ice Land.
- The household draws its measures at the **water tally** (*ubao-maji*, H) of the quarter (N), and the keeper writes each one.
- A house has **two jars**. The sweet jar is sealed, and it stands against the nightward wall, where it is cool. The used jar takes all water that has served once (*maji-pili*, second water). It goes back to the hydroloop (C09) or to the plants. No water is thrown on the ground.

## The Stair of Uses

Water goes down this stair and never up it.

1. Drinking and cooking.
2. Hands.
3. The body.
4. Cloth.
5. The floor and the plants.

## Washing

- **Hands.** One person pours from a small jug over the hands of another, above a basin, for about three breaths. The basin goes to the used jar. This is done before every meal, and the youngest person at the table pours.
- **The body.** A cloth and one cup, each day. Oil and a scraper take off the dust.
- **Teeth.** A chewing stick (*mswaki*). No water.
- **The steam house** (*hamamu*). In the north and in the caves steam costs nothing (C11), and the Fire Land has baths (G). A heartland town has a steam house in each quarter, heated by the thermal exchanger. A person goes once in a few days. It is the place where news passes.
- **Hair** is braided close to the head or wrapped in a scarf (N). This keeps the dust out and needs little water. Braiding is done in the court, in the lee, and it takes hours of talk.
- **Clothes** are beaten, aired in the wind and hung on the lit wall. They are seldom washed, and wool almost never.
- **The latrine** is dry. What it holds goes to the recyclers (C09).

## Drinking and Pouring

- People drink little plain water. Most of the fluid of a day is thin porridge, chai and light grain ferment (C11).
- The cup is small (C11). A host fills it to two thirds, because a full cup spills.
- To spill is a fault. To spill and not see it is a worse one. When water is spilled the room is quiet for one breath, and nobody makes a joke until the person who spilled it does.
- The first test of a small child is to carry a full cup across the court in the wind.

## The Sound of Water

- People know the sound of their pipes. A drip wakes a sleeper.
- Anyone may knock on any door, at any bell, to report a joint that drips.
- After a line of storm the gutters run into the cisterns. People stop work to listen to it.

## Gifts and Secrets

- ***Sadaka*** is a gift of water with no witness (C06). A sealed jar stands at a door when the house wakes. Nobody asks who put it there.
- **The old jar.** The Thirst Wars were about twenty Ancestor Cycles ago (C08). Many people who are old enough to remember the embargo keep one sealed jar more than their measure. By the letter of the law this is hoarding, and the word for it is *kinabara* (H). Their families know and do not speak of it.

# The Day

## The Bells

A settlement rings five bells, and the first is *sori* (C06). The rest period begins at *kiza* (H). In the novel a day has ten bells: five in the waking period and five in the rest period (N). This document reads the two together.

- The **five day bells** (*kengele-kuu*) are loud. At each one many people stop for one breath and one word of thanks (C06).
- The **five rest bells** (*kengele-pole*) are one soft stroke each. They are for the watch.
- A bell is also a length of time: "for the rest of the bell" (N). It is a little less than two and a half hours.
- Duskara has clocks and watches (*saa*, H). The bell house keeps a clock, its keeper rings by it, and many people carry a watch. Hours and minutes serve for short things (N, G). The bell stays the measure of the shared day: a watch tells one person the hour, and a bell tells everybody at once.
- A day is ten bells. It is as long as a day of Earth, near enough: a Cycle has not quite thirty-five of them (N).

| Bell | Hours after *sori* | What it marks |
|------|-------------------:|---------------|
| First of the day (*sori*) | 0 | Work begins. It is not the bell of waking: people are up before it (N). |
| Second | about 2.5 | Markets open (C03). Lessons begin (N). |
| Third | about 5 | The middle of the work. Shifts change. |
| Fourth | about 7 | The meal (N, C03). |
| Fifth, the last bell | about 9.5 | The public day ends. Halls close and children go down. In the Cycle of *saumu*, people eat after it (C06). |
| First of the rest (*kiza*) | 12 | First sleep. |
| Second of the rest | about 14.5 | |
| Third of the rest | about 17 | The middle wake. |
| Fourth of the rest | about 19 | Second sleep. |
| Fifth of the rest | about 21.5 | Rising: the cloth and the cup, thin porridge, chai, the walk to work. |

The old words survive for the parts of the waking period (C03): morning from the first bell to the second, midday to the third, afternoon to the fourth, evening after it.

## Sleep

- **Darkness is made.** The sleeping room is the lowest and darkest room of the house, cut into the ground, where it is also warm and quiet (C06). People say that they "go down" for sleep and "come up" for waking. Where the rock does not allow a lower room, sleeping rooms have shutters.
- On the road a sleeper ties a cloth over the eyes (*nguo-lala*), and a caravan carries a bell.
- **Many people sleep twice** (C03). The first sleep is four or five hours. Then comes the **middle wake** (*kesha*), at about the third rest bell: a quiet hour of low talk, of partners, of mending, of a word to the wind, of a look at the child. Then the second sleep. Other people sleep through, and nobody remarks on either habit.
- A town never sleeps all at once. The watch and the rest shift keep the works (C03).
- A child has no dark outside to tell it that the day is over. It learns to sleep by the bell, and a child who "will not go down" is the common trouble of parents.
- **Out of bell** (*nje-kengele*). A person whose sleep has left the bells is in some trouble: grief, sickness, or a new ability (N). Neighbors see the lamp and hear the steps. It is often the first thing that a witness sees (C06).
- A watch tells the hour and does not make a person sleepy. A person alone, with no bell and no company, drifts: the body makes a longer day. A lone traveler comes into a town "a bell late" and needs two days to come back to the count.

## Rest Days

By old custom a person rests one day in seven, as the caravans do (G). The rota sets the day for each person, so a town never stops all at once. The span of seven days has no name.

## Longer Time

- No part of the Cycle is warm and no part is cold. The weather has **phases** (C10): the Long Dry (C10), a Storm Phase (G), phases of cold wind and of warm wind (C06, C13). People feel them in the lips, the joints and their sleep.
- A farming town counts the Cycle by its grain. People say "at this point of the Cycle" of a crop and of a river (N).
- People date their own lives by storms, by the heading of the grain (N), by festivals and by births. The count of Cycles is for contracts and records (C10).
- A child's age is given in Cycles and an adult's in Ancestor Cycles (N).

# The House and the Lane

## The Common House

This pattern follows from the wind and the light. Rich houses and city blocks change it, but they keep its order.

- **The back** is the nightward wall: thick, low, with no opening. The wind and the rain strike it. The gutter and the cistern are at its foot, and the sweet jar stands against it inside. A weatherworking house also turns its back to the night (N).
- **The roof** is low at the back and rises toward the day. Nets of cable or rows of stones hold it down (N), and it hums.
- **The face** is the dayward wall: the door, the high windows, and a bench. It is lit, and it is in the lee.
- **The court** lies on the dayward side (C06). It holds the hearth (*jiko*), the porridge crock, the loom, the drying lines, and in small places the goats. The houses of one kin share a court: this is the compound (C06).
- **Below** are the store and the sleeping room (C06).
- **Inside**: mats and low stools. A shelf with the cups of the family, each one different (C04). In many houses, most of all in the north, a shelf of name tablets (C06). Wrap cloths with their sayings (C06), hung as curtains. Tools on pegs, each one carved (C06).

## The House Stays

A house belongs to a line and stays in it. People are born and die in the same compound, and a house holds the cups, the tablets and the carvings of many generations. A Belter who hears that the Deepkin change rooms by rota (C12) does not know what to say.

## Fuel

The surface has little to burn. The only wood is stormroot, and a council releases it for beams and drums, not for fire (G).

- **The hearth burns cake** (*kuni*, H): bricks pressed from grain straw, the waste of sunspire stalks and goat dung, and dried on the lit wall. It burns slowly, with a sour smoke. This is the smoke of the lanes.
- **The collective kitchens and the kiln of the dead burn gas** from the recyclers (C09).
- **A hot plate** runs on the power quota of the house.
- **Much heat is not fire:** the thermal exchangers (C09), the geothermal crystals that give back stored heat for a whole phase (C06), and the vents of the Fire Land, which need no fuel (G).
- **Lamp oil** is pressed from the seed crops (C11).
- A flame is small and watched. A large open fire is a feast or it is waste.

## The Door

- A visitor stops outside and calls *hujambo*. The house answers *karibu* (H).
- Shoes come off. A hand brush hangs by the door, and the visitor brushes the dust from coat and hair before they step in.
- The cup comes before any business (C11).
- A cloth tied to the ring of a door means that the house wants to be left alone, and neighbors respect it (C04).

## The Lanes

- **Cross lanes** run from the night to the day. The wind and the light go down them together. Builders make them so, to lead the wind through a city (C06). They are bright and loud, and they hold the turbines and the carts.
- **Long lanes** run along the belt. They are calm and dim. The doors, the stalls and the children are there.
- Markets stand in courts that the wind does not reach (C03).
- A town climbs in terraces where it can, so that each row sees over the next toward the day (G).
- Carvings cover the windward walls (C06). At the height of a hand they are worn smooth.
- **What a lane sounds like:** the hum, the bells, the drum (C15), goats, a loom, children who say a survival poem in chorus (C18), and from the cricket sheds (C11) a steady song. Some children keep one cricket in a small cage of fiber, for its voice.

# Food

The Compendium gives the foods (C11). This chapter gives the table.

## Through the Day

| When | What | Where |
|------|------|-------|
| Before the first bell | Thin sour porridge (*uji*), warm, and chai (C11) | At the hearth of the house |
| During work | Flatbread in a cloth (N), bean paste, dried fruit, roasted pods of windvine (C17) | Where the work is |
| The fourth bell | Sima, stew, pickle, a little fish or meat (C11) | The hall of the compound or the quarter (C03) |
| After the meal | Light ferment; wind-wine at feasts (C11); stories (C03) | The hall |

## At the Table

- Hands are washed by pouring. The water-blessing comes before the food (C11).
- **The right hand eats.** A person pinches off a piece of sima, rolls it, presses a hollow in it with the thumb, and takes up the stew with it (N).
- **The cook serves and the cook counts** (N). Nobody takes for themselves.
- **Nobody leaves food in a bowl** (N). A person who cannot finish hands the bowl to a neighbor, who empties it and says nothing about it.
- The collective kitchen cooks once for many people and saves fuel (C03). The hearth of a house makes chai and porridge and warms what was cooked elsewhere.

## Taste

- **Sour comes first.** Fermented food keeps with no fuel and no cold (C11). A Duskaran child knows sour before it knows any other taste, because the first food is the thin porridge (C11).
- Then salt, from the salt flats of the Dayward Edge (G), then smoke, then the bitter leaf of the herb teas (C11).
- **Fresh green leaf** from the growing towers is the small luxury of an ordinary day. Fresh fruit is rare.
- Feast food is rice (C11), goat, fresh fish and wind-wine.
- Food is enough and seldom more. Portions follow the work (C11), and a person who has done a hard shift is served first.

## By Region

- The north and the caves steam their food, because steam is free there (C11). Some old crew lines of the north still eat with two sticks at feasts.
- The cities of the lake eat fresh fish. Rice grows only at Mdomo-Luŋga (G), and it stays a feast food (C11).
- The Fire Land cooks on the vents (C06).
- Fried crickets are sold in the markets of poor quarters and of the Dry Land (C11), in a folded leaf.

# The Body and Its Cover

## Clothes

- Layers that can be opened and closed (C03): a wrap cloth, a coat, trousers, boots, a scarf (N).
- Wind-wear (*hanga-vazi*, H) is for open ground: tight cuffs, tied ankles, a hood.
- Fiber comes from the stalk of the sunspire (C17) and from the hair of the goats that every town keeps (C11). The shed fur of the ember stalker goes into coats for the cold (C17).
- **The wrap cloth** (*kanga*) **carries a saying** (C06). The saying is chosen with care. A cloth that is given as a gift says what the giver will not say aloud, and the person who receives it reads it twice.
- **Marks that others read:** the sash of adulthood (C04), the ash of a calling (N), a bond-token (C05), the blue-gray of a water judge (C04).

## The Body

- Brown skin in many shades, and hair of every kind (C03). A Belter has a reflective layer in the eye (C05), and in a dim room it can catch the lamp.
- **Loads go on the back,** with a band across the shoulders or on a frame (*kiunzi*) (N). A person who walks uphill with empty hands, when something has to go up, hears about it (N).
- **Hands are not idle.** A person who sits carves, spins or knots. This is why every tool, cup and doorpost is carved (C06), and why a child has a small carved thing in a pocket (N).
- **People touch.** A hand on the back, on the neck, on the face (N). Any adult of the compound picks up any child (C04).
- **People point with the chin or the lips.** A finger that points at a person is an accusation.
- **What is in a pocket:** a knife, a length of cord, a chewing stick, a carved thing, and at council time a wind-token (C04).

## Gender

- **The language does not sort people.** Creole has one pronoun for a third person, *ŋa* (H), and a personal name fits anyone (C19). Only a few kin words carry a gender: *ama*, *bapa*, *bibi* (H).
- **Work does not sort them either.** The rota looks at skill and at who is free (C04). Clothes, hair and seats on a council are the same for all.
- **Gender is still there.** People have one, most say it with a kin word or not at all, and a body matters to the healer and in a pregnancy. Some settlements have named roles for people who are neither or both, and each has its own words for them (C04).
- **Care.** A healer (*tiba-ji*) treats the alignment of a body with the gender of the person as ordinary care (C04). People speak of it as they speak of any cure: the person is coming to *sawa*.
- **What changes in the house** is one word. The family begins to say *bapa* where it said *ama*, or the reverse, and by custom the youngest says it first, at the meal.
- A stranger does not ask, and does not guess aloud.

## Small Ills

- Cracked lips and knuckles. Eyes that run. Ears that ache. A dry cough in dust phases. One stiff shoulder, on the side that takes the wind on the road to work.
- Chilblains toward the night and heat sickness toward the day.
- Thermal sensors have migraines (C05). Weatherworkers have nosebleeds and a shake in the hands (C05).
- The healer is the *tiba-ji* or the *dok* (H). The common cures are herb teas and fungal salves (C06), frostbloom tea for the cold (C17), the sap of the warmedge cactus on a wound (C17), and glowcap for the chest (C17).

# Manners

The Creole word is *adabu* (H).

## Meeting and Leaving

- In the heartland a greeting is short. On the caravan roads it is long: family, water, road, and then the business (H, N).
- The farewell is *hanga-salama*, safe winds (H).
- An elder is addressed as *bapa* or *ama* (H).
- At each day bell a person may stop in the middle of a sentence for one breath (C06). The other person waits and does not take it as a slight.
- A person who prays turns to face the wind (C06). So prayer is done with the back to the light and the face in the cold.

## In the Wind

- **Give your lee.** A person who stops in the open with an elder, a child or someone under a load steps to their nightward side and takes the wind on the back.
- **Do not stand in another person's wind.** To block the wind of a drying line, a hearth or a kite is the small form of a great offense (C18), and the answer is "you are in my wind".
- A person who must pass close goes by on the dayward side, so that their dust does not fall on the other.

## About Water and Food

- Pour low. Close the jar. Empty the bowl.
- Take the cup that is offered (C11, N).
- Never say aloud how much water another person holds, and never ask.

## About Counting

- To say aloud what a person has carried is polite, and it is expected (N).
- To say aloud what a person failed to carry is an accusation, and it belongs before a council (C04).

## Speech

- A careful speaker says how they know a thing: seen, heard or sensed (H). People do it in gossip too. News without one of these words is taken as seen, so a person who passes on a rumor as plain fact has lied twice.
- In council a person asks permission before they speak (H). Things are talked out until the group agrees (C06), and this is slow. People bring their carving.
- The great insults are about waste. They are almost never spoken: people use a roundabout phrase or a hand sign (C18).
- **Humor** lives on counting, on loads and on the wind (N). People tease the ones they like. A stranger who is teased has been accepted.

## Privacy

- People are seldom alone. To work alone is the thing that every guild fears (C05), and the feeling reaches beyond the guilds: a person who keeps apart for long is watched with some care.
- The answer to a crowd is not a wall. It is to stand side-on and let it go past (N).
- Neighbors do not see what a tied door hides (C04). They do see a person who has gone out of bell.
- A person with an ability is always partly elsewhere (W). Nobody asks them what the air is doing while they eat.

# Work

- Every able person gives time to the works of the settlement: turbines, cisterns, routes (C04). A water judge takes a turn on the cistern crew like any other person.
- The **rota** (*zamu*, H) sets the turns. A shift is about two bells. Most people work one shift at their trade and give part of another to the household or the rota.
- **Everything is counted** (N): loads, bowls, empty sacks, rows of grain, measures of water. There is a slate or a tally board at every door where goods pass.
- Children scrape the scale from the condensers every seven days (C06). It is their first work for the town, and they are proud of it.
- The market is open from the second bell to the third (C03). Much of the trade is by hand sign (C18), and a deal closes on *nzuri* (H).
- A person is paid in rations and in Windmarks, which stand for water, work and power (C11).

## What Every Child Can Do

By about ninety Cycles, when lessons begin (C04), a child can:

- tie down a load so that it stays;
- pour without loss;
- read the nightward sky for a line that is coming (C02);
- make the hand signals and speak the storm register (H);
- find the nearest shelter from any place in the town;
- say the survival poems of the house (C18);
- keep a tally (N).

## The Dead

The body is dried, so that its water returns to the settlement, and then it is burned (C04).

1. **The vigil.** The body lies for one rest period in the lower room of its house. The kin oil it; they use no water. The door stands open for that rest period, against all other custom, and anyone may come down.
2. **The drying.** Keepers carry the body to the drying house, at the dayward end of the town. Its water goes back into the loop. People say that the dead person "has given back the water". The keeper of the tally strikes the name at that bell.
3. **The burning.** A kiln does it, on recycler gas, or on the heat of a vent in the Fire Land.
4. **The ash.** The kin carry it to a place where three currents meet. They face the wind, speak the name (C04), and open the hand. The ash goes dayward.
5. **The tablet.** Houses that keep name tablets add one (C06).
6. **The name.** Nobody in the close family carried it while the person lived (C19). Now it is free, and the next child may have it.

- **Mourning.** The house ties an undyed cloth to the ring of its door for one Cycle. It does not light its hearth for seven days, and the neighbors bring cooked food. A parent who loses a child has leave from the rota (C04).
- **A person whom a storm took and did not give back.** The kin speak the name and open an empty hand.
- **The Deepkin** have no wind. They burn their dead at a vent. The Bone Lorekeepers (C07) keep one small piece of burned bone from each of the dead in the wall of the Hearth, and they say the names (C07).
- A bonded animal has its own rite (C05).

## The Council Floor

- Any member of the settlement may speak in an open forum (C04). A speaker asks permission (H) and says how they know what they report (H).
- The things under discussion lie in the circle: a map, a tool, a token (C04).
- In a forum a person votes with a smooth stone to keep things as they are, or with a carved wind-token to change them (C04). A sitting council raises hands (C08).
- A forum is long, because things are talked out (C06). People bring their carving, and children sleep against the wall. A person who leaves before the end gives the reason aloud.
- A group of citizens can call an emergency audience, and the council must answer within three days (C08).
- Workers petition against a guild and families against an allocation (C12). A young person can appeal against their own parents (C04).
- Seats turn with the wind phases (C08), so most adults know somebody who has sat.

## News

- The drum relay (G), the bell, the flute of a caravan (C06), the Lorekeeper in the hall (C03), the tally and the steam house.
- A report carries its witness word (H). "Heard" news from the steam house is repeated as heard.

## Standing

- A person stands by what they carry (C04). The rota board is public, so work is seen. Water is not seen: nobody says what another person holds.
- Lines that hold water rights and lines with an ability have weight (C04, C12). So has the end of the town where a family lives.
- Marks of standing are few: the sash, the robe of a judge (C04), the numbered grade of a guild (C06).
- The worst thing is to be named for waste (C18). It is rare, and it goes before the council. The common penalty for a small fault is small and public: extra turns on the rota.
- There is no police (C08). There are the safety teams, who are neighbors (C13), the witness (C06), and the Wayseer who mediates (C12).
- A family that cannot live with its council moves to another town (C12).

## Guests

- A stranger is *kinya* and a guest is *mgeni* (H). A traveler gets the cup (C11), and then the keeper of the tally writes a guest measure.
- A person is a guest for three days. On the fourth the rota board carries their name.

## Together

- In a storm every person has a place in a shelter and a task. The town drills for it (C13), and after the storm the whole town repairs (C13).
- When a house must be moved or built again, the lane lifts and carries without payment (C06).
- At a feast the hall answers the storyteller (C15), and nobody only watches.

# Courtship

Canon gives the frame. Courtship is informal and personal. People meet at gatherings, on work details, or through a friend or a mentor (C04). A person marries outside their own line (C06). Consent is taught to everyone at the Threshold (C04).

## Signs

- **Lee to an equal.** To give lee (*kinga*) to an elder is manners. To step to the nightward side of a person of your own age, who does not need it, is a declaration. If they stay in it, that is an answer.
- **The load.** To offer to take part of another person's load is a question. To be allowed is the first yes.
- **Counting.** A person who is interested remembers what the other carried, and says so.
- **The cloth.** A *kanga* with a chosen saying is the gift of courtship. To wear it is yes. To give it back folded is no, and nothing more is said.
- **A carved thing** for the pocket.
- **Hair.** To braid the hair of another person in the open court tells the whole lane.
- **The cup.** To pour for a person out of turn.
- **The dance.** Partners in the wind-dance often become partners in life (C04).

## Rules

- **Ask in words, and ask once.** The opening is *Ni sema wazi ke?*, "may I speak openly?" (H). A no is taken as it is given. A second ask is pressure. After a third, a friend of the other person comes to speak, as a witness does (C06).
- **Never court with water.** Nor with food. A gift of either with a wish behind it is the first step of a crime (C04). This is one reason why *sadaka* has no witness.
- **The first two questions are the line and the rota.** Two people of one line cannot marry (C06), and two people on opposite shifts will seldom meet.
- **A person with an ability says so early.** People fear emotional weather (C05), and a careful person with an ability keeps the first meetings in company.
- **Words.** *Mpenzi*, beloved, can bind as well as please (H). *Mahaba* is tenderness, and *upendo-huru* is love that is freely given (H).

## Places

- The lee of a wall on its lit side. The long lanes. The steps of the steam house. A roof, where there is more sun. The walk home from the hall after the fourth bell.
- Lovers in two towns write by caravan, ten to twelve days each way between neighbor cities (G).
- The marriage caravan (C04) cuts across all of this. A person who goes with it may leave somebody behind. See The Marriage Caravan.

# Married Life

## The Bond

- An everyday partnership needs no rite (C04). Many couples mark it with the Wind's Embrace at a wind focal point (C04).
- A formal partnership is *ndoa*. Its terms are traded first: this is *shanda* (C04). They are written and witnessed when children or resources are part of it (C04). The ceremony waits for a good wind, the partners exchange fans (C06), and the feast is the *arusi*.
- **The trade covers:** which house; the measures of water; the rota; children, and the request for a birth authorization; what each owes to the kin of the other; if others are allowed (C04); how it ends (C04).
- **An alliance marriage** between settlements is a public act (C04). A Wayseer records the consent of each person (C04). The partners may be near strangers, and they learn each other afterward.

## The House

- A house belongs to a line and stays in it. So one partner moves, and the trade says which one.
- The one who moves keeps their line name and their Wind-Kin (C19). A child takes the line of either parent and the clan of one (C19).
- The one who moves comes into a compound: elders, cousins, a shared court (C06). The couple has the lower room and the tied door.
- The house draws its measures together, under one mark at the tally.
- The two fans hang side by side on the wall. They carry the history of both lines (C06).

## The Ordinary Day

- Partners on different shifts meet at the meal and in the middle wake (*kesha*).
- **The burden talk** is normal and is not a crisis: "I feel that the burden of the house is not balanced. Can we trade again?" (H).
- **Renewal** (*shanda-upya*). A partnership is a choice that is made again (C04). At each anniversary (C04) the partners sit before a few friends, say aloud what each one carries, and trade again. Each adds a mark to their fan.
- Many adults raise one child (C04): a parent of a partner, a sibling, a friend of the house. The parent is the person who does the care (C04).
- Partners ask for a birth together, and then they wait for the council (C04). The wait is a known strain on a house.

## Trouble

- The compound hears everything. The first help is a witness (C06).
- A partner can ask the kin leader or the council to review the bond. A Wayseer mediates, and a shared token can be held by the kin leader while they talk (C04).
- People speak of a bond under strain as a vessel under pressure (C04).

## The End

- A bond that is not renewed has ended, and the contract says how the goods and the care are divided (C04).
- The partner who moved in goes back to the house of their own line, which has kept the place. Each fan goes back to its line.
- An end that was traded fairly carries no shame. Shame is for the person who left the other one carrying.

## The Marriage Caravan

The *baŋga'sora* joins lines and settlements across the arc (C04). It has a rule, and it has a practice.

**The rule**

- **The roster.** It hangs at the hall. A person writes their own name on it, before the kin leader or the Wayseer, and can strike it with the same hand until the caravan leaves (C04).
- **The first terms** are fixed with the home council before departure: which settlements, how many Cycles away at most, the right to come home with no bond, and letters carried without charge (C04).
- **The Wayseer asks each person alone,** at each halt, and asks once.
- **A pairing** needs two yeses. Each person says it to the Wayseer alone, and then both say it together. A no needs no reason (C04). The Wayseer writes "no alliance" and does not write who refused.
- **The second terms** are the trade of the two partners (*shanda*): the house, the length of the stay, the letters, the return (C04). Many caravan bonds are made for a stated number of Cycles and renewed like any other.
- **It is not love, and nobody says that it is.** The bond is a *ndoa* (C04). If the trade allows it, a partner keeps a beloved at home (C04).
- **Two people of the same sex** pair on the caravan as any others do. Such a bond joins two lines, opens a road between two towns, or makes a house that will raise a child (C04).
- **A caravan bond brings no birth authorization by itself.** The town that receives the partner says in the terms if its quota has room (C04).

**The practice**

- A place on the roster is called an honor, and in a poor settlement it is often a price (C04). A council that needs water or a weatherworker finds people who will "choose" to go, and they are most often people without an ability (C04).
- **The signs** are known: a roster where every name comes from the same end of the town; a name in a hand that is not the person's own; a family whose ration improved in the same Cycle.
- Here the witness has work (C06). A Wayseer who doubts can refuse to take a name.

**The burden**

- The people who leave and the people who stay carry a grief, and it is called by the plain word for a load, *mzigo* (C04). The lane takes the turns of the one who left for one Cycle.
- **Praise comes after, not before.** Nobody tells a person that it is brave to go, or grown-up, until the name is on the roster by their own hand. Praise before the choice is pressure.
- A letter between the ends of the arc and the Bahari is about four Cycles on the road by caravan, and about twenty days by Whisper Road (G).

## Other Shapes

Three or four partners, with every term stated (C04). Two friends who share a house and a child with no romance (C04). Partners who do not want sex, and who join for work and care (C04).

# Sex

Canon gives the frame. It is a need like food and sleep. It carries no shame and no secrecy, and it rests on honesty and consent (C04). Partners of the same sex have the same standing as any others (C04).

## Custom

- **The three truths** (*kweli-tatu*). Before the first time, each person says three things: the state of their health, their agreements with other people, and their contraception. A lie in any of the three is the offense that canon names (C04).
- **Consent is spoken**, and it can be taken back at any breath (C04).
- **Nobody gives water or food for it** (C04).
- **A thermal sensor** reads fear and wanting in the heat of a skin (C05). By custom such a person says that they can, once, at the start.
- To be exclusive is a term of the trade and is not assumed (C04). People ask.

## Where and When

- The lower room, the middle wake, the tied door (C04).
- **Quiet rooms.** A city has rooms for people who have no privacy at home (C04). They stand beside the steam house. Nobody pays and nobody gives a name. A cloth on the ring shows that a room is in use.
- A small settlement has only timing and tact (C04).

## The Body

- People go to the steam house first, if they can. Oil does the rest.
- The room is cold, so warmth is part of the pleasure. The word is *starehe*: comfort, pleasure and closeness in one (H).

## Birth Control

Duskara plans its growth (C03, C04). These rules follow from that.

- **Contraception is a drug** (*dawa-zuia*, H). The healer gives it to any adult who asks, and it does not count against a ration. To take it is the normal state, and it is taught at the Threshold (C04).
- **Abortion** (*toa-mimba*, H) **is the choice of the mother.** She asks the healer. She needs no permission from a partner, a kin leader or a council, and nobody asks her for a reason.
- **A pregnancy without a birth authorization** (*ruhusa-zaa*, H) goes before the council.
  - The council can grant the authorization late, if the quota of the settlement has room.
  - The council can let the pregnancy go on without it. Then the household loses rations, and another household, one that holds an authorization, raises the child.
  - The council can order an abortion, if it is necessary.
- People know that this is hard. It is the rule that makes no exceptions (C12), applied to the thing that people love most. A forced abortion is the gravest act that a council can order: it is rare, and nobody who sat on such a council forgets it.
- The child who is raised by another household is a full child of that house (C04). It keeps no mark, and most towns do not tell.

## Talk

- People joke about it in the steam house, without malice and without names.
- Jealousy exists. It goes into the burden talk like any other load.

## In the Caves

Warm pools (C03), a strong sense of privacy (C07), and the dark. Fingers with glowing marks (C06) can say what the Deepkin do not say aloud.

# How It Feels

## The Ground Notes

- **Balance.** The highest praise for a person, a meal or a plan is *sawa*. A thing can be too generous or too bright as well as too mean or too dark.
- **To be counted is to be cared for.** The cook who counts the bowls will know who did not eat (N). A person whom nobody counts is a person whom nobody would miss. Children like to be counted, and lonely people stand near a tally.
- **Enough.** The good state is not plenty. It is a full cistern, a sealed jar and a crock that works. The sound of a full cistern is the sound of peace.
- **Carrying.** Pride is in what a person carries for others (C04). "She carries" is a full compliment. The shame of the hoarder (C18) is the reverse of it.
- **Hidden kindness.** The best gift has no witness (C06). So people are plain and even hard in what they say, and soft in what they do when nobody looks. A Duskaran who is thanked in public is uneasy.
- **The rule that makes no exceptions** (C12). Everybody has seen it hurt somebody. It gives people a reserve: they do not promise what the measure may not allow, and they do not ask it of others.

## Comfort and Fear

| Comfort | Fear |
|---------|------|
| A lee, with the wind loud overhead | Still air |
| The roof that hums | A roof that goes quiet |
| The bells, and people at each one | A person out of bell; a lamp at the wrong hour |
| The hall at the fourth bell, when everybody talks | To work alone (C05) |
| The gutters that run after a line | The sound of a drip |
| Hot chai in cold hands | A smell from the day side |
| The steam house | To be lost: mist, a cave, a closed room |
| A known cold step and a known warm corner | A known place that has changed |

## The Two Sides

- **The sun is respected and not loved.** It is the Lion. It does not blink and it does not go away. The day side stands for force and for ambition that cannot be reached (C03). Every seat faces the day, so people have the Lion before them for all of their lives, and they do not look at it: the eyes rest lower, on the court and on the work.
- **The night is where things come from.** Water, wind, and the dead who live in the wind (C06). It is cold and it is not evil. A person who turns to pray turns toward it.
- **The two edges are always in view,** fire one way and ice the other, each within seven days on foot. People think of them as others think of a mountain above their town: seldom, and never without respect.

## Time

- Nothing in the world says that it is late. Only the bells say it, and the bells are other people. So time is company. A person who keeps the bells is among people, even alone in a room.
- Memory holds to places more than to times. A place does not change its light or its cold, so a person who goes back to where a thing happened finds it as it was.
- "Forward's the only direction that makes sense" (C18). People do not make much of what they have reached. They ask whether a person is still going.

## People

- **Children are few and public.** A birth needs an authorization (C04), so a child is the pride of a whole lane. A child is never alone and never out of sight, and when it grows it must learn to want that.
- **The old remember the embargo** (C08, C12). The young have heard of it and are tired of hearing. This is the usual line of a quarrel in a family.
- **The young look along the belt.** Stability bores them (C12), and the Far Edge is the place of their talk (G).
- **Earth is a word.** Nobody is homesick for it (C06).
- **Those with an ability** are honored, and people are careful near them (C05). Their neighbors watch them for the first signs of harm, and they know that they are watched.

## A Good Day

The grain is heading. The cistern is full. Nobody at the table is missing. The roof hums. There is green leaf with the sima, and someone tells the old story badly, and everyone corrects them.

## What Changes on a Journey

- **Dayward:** the sun stands higher, the air is warmer and drier, shadows shorten, and the grit begins.
- **Nightward:** the sun sinks, the air is colder and wetter, the ground rises, and the ground itself begins to glow.
- **Up-belt:** colder, windier, more rain, more words from the crew, thicker walls.
- **Down-belt:** drier, dustier, less water in the cup, longer greetings.
- Along the belt the sun stays at the same height for the whole journey. Across the belt it changes with each day of walking. A traveler who has crossed the belt has seen the sun move, and few other people ever do.

# Under the Night

The life of the Deepkin (*pango-jata*), from the inside.

- **Light.** It comes from living things: the blue-green of glowcaps (C17), murals that grow (C07), markers on the walls (C09). It comes from the floor and the walls as often as from above, so a face is lit from beneath. Nothing has a fixed shadow. Near a vent the rock itself is a dim red (C00).
- **Air.** Warm, damp and still (C03). Hair lies flat. A flame stands straight.
- **Smell.** Mineral water, fungus, a little sulfur (C03); steam from the kitchens; warm stone; many people in one place. There is no smoke: food is steamed or cooked on the vents.
- **Sound.** Every sound goes far and comes back (C03). People speak low and slowly, and they hold their long vowels (H). A person is known by their step. Stone bells carry signals (G) and chimes ring in the warm air that rises (C15). For some work nobody speaks at all, and fingers with glowing marks do the talking (C06). In the outer tunnels the silence is complete.
- **Warmth is the measure of a place.** Near the Hearth it is warm. In the outer caverns it is cold (C12). Families change rooms by rota so that nobody keeps the cold for all time (C12). People own little, and they belong to the Hearth more than to a room.
- **Time.** The caves keep the same count of ten bells, by stone bell and chime, because trade needs one count.
- **Water** is warm. It comes from steam (C09). People sit in the pools (C03) and are cleaner than any Belter, and they are as sparing with every other thing (C07).
- **Clothes** are light (C03) and carry dyes that glow (C03).
- **The table:** pale fungi with a taste of nuts, blind fish, algae (C03), the shellfish of the hot pools (C17), young fronds of the geotherm fern (C17), buns (C11).
- **Company.** Glowmoss grazers are kept near the houses (C17). A guest is received with care and trusted late (C07).

# Crossing Over

**A Belter below.** For the first days the body waits for the storm, because the air is still. There is no day side, so there is no direction, and some people are sick with it. The silence stops their sleep. The warmth feels heavy. Their red cloth looks black in the green light, and their voice is too loud for every room. Then the ears open, and the warm water does the rest.

**A Deepkin above.** The sky has no roof. Many keep their eyes on the ground and walk close to walls. The wind is a hand on the body that never lets go, and one side of the face is always cold. The red light is hard on eyes that are made for the dark (C03), and they cover them. The cloth that glowed at home is dull. Nothing is ever quiet, and the hum of the roof stops their sleep. They find the people loud, dusty and careless of what they have (C08).

Each side looks dull and dark in the light of the other.

# At the Gate

A trade day at a gate of the Deep Roads (G), as at Motowan.

- **The mouth.** The air of the tunnel is warm and damp, and the wind of the belt is cooler and drier. Where they meet there is mist. From far off, the mouth of a Deep Road is a white plume that leans dayward.
- **Smell.** Mineral water, fungus and sulfur come up from below (C03) and meet dust and lamp oil. A Belter smells the caves before the first Deepkin comes into view.
- **Two lamps.** The gate hall is dim, because the red light is hard on eyes that are made for the dark (C03). Glowcap jars stand on the cave side and oil lamps on the belt side. A color is not the same under each lamp, so a buyer carries cloth or dye across the hall before anybody says a price. "Show it under both lamps" is also what people say when they doubt a story.
- **Sound.** The Deepkin speak low and slowly (H), and the Belters are too loud for them. Much of the trade is by hand sign (C18). The Deepkin add the glowing marks on their fingers (C06). A deal closes on *nzuri* (H).
- **The bells.** Both sides keep the same ten bells. The stone bell in the tunnel answers the bell house of the city, one stroke late.
- **What comes up:** ice packed in straw, minerals, dried fungi and blind fish (C11), glowing dyes (C03), geothermal crystals in double crates (C06).
- **What goes down:** grain, cloth, tools and forged parts (C08).
- **The judge.** A water judge tests each load of ice before it goes on (G) and reads the tokens that come with it (C04).
- **The cup.** Each side gives the cup to the other before trade begins (C11). The Deepkin receive a guest with care and trust late (C07): a trader who has come to the same gate for twenty Cycles still hears the same polite questions.
- **The envoy's table.** A Wind and Flame Envoy (C08) sits between the two sides. When a dispute is open, the thing in dispute lies on that table, as it would lie in the circle of a council (C04). Trade goes on around it.
- **How it feels.** Each side finds the other a little rude and does not say so. The Belter thinks that the Deepkin answer too slowly. The Deepkin think that the Belter wastes words and brings dust (C08). The children of both sides stare. After the last bell each side goes home pleased with the price, and sure that the other one was difficult.

# For the Writer

## Before a Scene

Decide these, and let two or three of them show.

1. Where is the night? Which cheek is cold?
2. Is there a lee? Who has it?
3. How much sun, and is it clear, veiled or shut?
4. Which bell was the last one?
5. What does the air bring from nightward?
6. What is in the hands?
7. Who is counting, and what?

## Ten Small Actions

1. A person turns a shoulder to the wind before they speak.
2. A person looks nightward as they come out of a door.
3. A person stops for one breath at a bell.
4. Shoes come off, and dust is brushed away at the door.
5. A cup is offered, and it is taken.
6. Someone pours with the spout on the rim.
7. Someone ties down the thing that they have just put down.
8. Sima is rolled and pressed with the thumb.
9. A load is counted aloud at a door.
10. A speaker says "seen" or "heard" before the news.

## She, He and Ŋa

Creole has one pronoun, *ŋa*, for every person (H). The English of the stories is a translation, and it uses "she", "he" or "they" as the character would say it of themselves (N). Do not make a character notice the gender of a job, a name or a garment: there is none to notice.
