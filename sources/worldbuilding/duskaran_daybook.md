# Introduction

This book is about the ordinary day on the Living Edge: what a person sees, hears, smells and touches between one bell and the next, what the hands do without thought, and what people feel about it. The Compendium describes how Duskara works, and the Gazetteer says where things are. Neither says what a kitchen sounds like when a pipe drips.

Most of the habits here follow from two facts: the wind always comes from one side, and the light always comes from the other. So the door is on the dayward wall, the sweet jar stands against the nightward one, and nobody is lost in the open in ordinary weather.

## The Ordinary Town

Unless a section says otherwise, what follows is true of a town of the heartland, in the nightward half of the Central band: cool, windy and grey, with enough water. Other places differ in degree more than in kind. The Four Lands and the Two Edges gives the differences, and Under the Night gives the caves.

# The Grain of the World

On the surface of Duskara everything has a direction, and the direction does not change.

| What | Comes from | Goes to |
|------|------------|---------|
| Light | The day side | |
| Heat | The day side | |
| Wind at the ground | The night side | The day side |
| Water | The ice of the night side | The salt flats of the Dayward Edge |
| The ground | High under the ice | Low toward the day side |
| The dead | They live in the wind | |

Light and heat come from one side. Wind, water and the ancestors come from the other. People live on the strip where the two cross.

- **Nobody is lost in ordinary weather.** A person who faces the light has the wind at the back. Grain, grass and smoke all lean the same way. To be lost is a thing of storms, mist, caves and closed rooms, and it frightens a Duskaran more than it would frighten a person of Earth.
- **Nightward is the hard way.** It is uphill, upwind and upstream, into the cold and the dark. Water comes from there. Dayward is downhill, downwind and downstream: the easy way, and the place where water ends.
- **The word for what is right is *sawa*, balanced.** The Central band is *Daŋa-Sawa*, the balanced edge. The blessing is *Heri hanga zi ja sawa*, "may your wind be balanced". Too much of the day and too much of the night are equal faults.
- **Directions serve at every size.** Creole has no east or west. It uses the four directions of the belt for small things too: the nightward shelf, the dayward stair, the nightward tower, the up-belt end of a table. Left and right are for the two hands and for nothing else.

## Figures of Speech

People talk in the grain of the world.

| Saying | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| It went dayward | It is lost or wasted and will not come back. Water that reaches the salt does not return. |
| To go nightward for it | To take the hard way; to go to the source of a matter. |
| Upwind of it | Before it; not yet touched by it. |
| Downwind of them | Living with the results of what other people do. |
| To give someone your lee (*toa kinga*) | To put yourself between a person and what is hard for them. |
| To sweep nightward | To do useless work. |
| Side-on | Not fighting a thing and not giving way to it. |

## What People Compare Things To

A Duskaran compares things to what they know: water in a channel (slack, current, set, weir); loads (to carry, to bear, to give way); the wind (lee, lean, line, side-on); counting (short, over, a tally that does not close); cloth, knots and cord; the grain (to head, to lie over, a slow row); stone and slate.

Some figures of Earth do not work here.

- **The sea.** The Bahari is the only sea. It has short waves and no tide, so nobody speaks of a tide that turns or of surf.
- **Light and dark.** Light is not good and dark is not bad. The day side burns and the night side gives water.
- **Return.** The sun does not move, and no part of the Cycle comes back in the weather. "After the rain, the sun" means nothing on the belt: the sun was there all along.
- **The striking hour.** A clock does not strike. The bell does, and the bell is people.

## The Exception of the Rift

In the rift of the heartland the river and the cold current of the floor run along the belt, not across it. Heartlanders are the only Duskarans who see water go sideways for a long distance. A person from another region who comes to the Bahari stands and looks at the river for some time.

# Light

## The Sun

- Its daily name is *sora*. The formal name is *simba-taiyo*, the lion-star, and people call it the Lion.
- It is a red dwarf. Its disc is about three times as wide as the sun of Earth: one finger at arm's length covers it. This follows from the mass of the star and the distance of the planet.
- Its color is that of an ember, between red and orange.
- **It does not move.** It stands at the dayward horizon, in the same place, for the whole of a life. A person can point to it with closed eyes.
- It gives light. It gives no warmth that the skin can feel, except on the Dayward Edge. A Duskaran is warmed by air, by walls and by the ground, not by the sun.
- Through the haze of the day wall it is dull enough to look at for one breath. Children are told not to.
- It flares. For some minutes a white point stands on the disc and the light turns hard. People then expect an aurora and bad signals within a day or two, and those who have an ability expect a bad head.

How much of the disc stands above the ground tells a person how far dayward they are.

| Where | Offset | On level ground with a clear horizon |
|-------|-------:|--------------------------------------|
| Nightward limit of the belt | -150 | A red glow, and at best the rim |
| Middle of the Nightward Edge | -100 | A low arc, about a quarter of the height of the disc |
| Nightward limit of the Central band | -50 | Half the disc |
| Center line | 0 | Most of the disc, cut at its foot |
| Dayward limit of the Central band | +50 | The whole disc, resting on the ground |
| Middle of the Dayward Edge | +100 | The whole disc, clear of the ground by a third of its width |
| Dayward limit of the belt | +150 | The whole disc, clear of the ground by more than half its width |

The Central band is the country where the ground cuts the sun. Nobody measures it: people judge it by eye, and a hill or the haze changes it everywhere.

- **Height shows more sun.** A tower, a roof or a scarp lifts the eye, and more of the disc comes into view. A climb of a hundred meters adds about a fifth of the disc. Children go up on roofs to see it.
- **A hill on the dayward side hides it for good.** Some houses, and some whole streets, have no sun and never will. Everybody knows which ones.

## The Sky

- **Dayward.** A band of copper and amber stands above the horizon, widest around the sun. This is the day wall, seen from far off: the dust and the heat that stand beyond the Dayward Edge. The sun is seen through it. It has three states. When it is **clear**, the disc is hard and red and every shadow has an edge. When it is **veiled**, which is the usual state in the Central band, the disc is a red stain in the copper and shadows are soft. When it is **shut**, in a dust phase, there is no disc for days: the light turns brown and nothing has a shadow.
- **Overhead.** Grey, with violet in it. The Creole word for grey is *hanga-rangi*, wind-color. It is the grey of every day in the heartland, and nobody there remarks on it.
- **Nightward.** Dark. From open ground a long low bank shows on that horizon and does not move: the night wall, over the ice. Its top takes the red of the sun and its foot is black. Lightning walks in it. From the floor of the rift, under the scarp, it is out of sight.
- **Aurora** (*anga-taa*). Green and red curtains above the night wall, after a flare. On the Nightward Edge they are common. In the Central band a strong one is an event, and a child born under it may carry its name.
- **Stars.** A few bright ones show low over the night in clear weather, and many more on the Nightward Edge. They drift through the Cycle, and the Archivists keep the count by them. Most people of the Central band cannot name one.
- **No moon.**
- **Cloud goes two ways.** Low cloud moves dayward with the wind. High cloud, and the dust of the day side, move nightward above it. Copper bands in a storm cloud are that dust coming down.

## Light on the Ground

- **Shadows do not move.** When the sun is clear they are long and they point nightward. Every wall has a lit face and a dark face for all time. Moss, damp and frost keep to the dark face. Things dry on the lit face.
- A person who walks nightward walks on their own shadow. A person who walks dayward has the light in the eyes: they pull a scarf or a brim forward, and lines come early at the corners of the eyes.
- **The light does not change with the bell.** It changes only with the weather: a line of storm, a dust phase, mist off the lake.
- **Color.** The low red light makes red, ochre, yellow and white strong. Blue and green look dark. Cloth and paint use white, red, ochre and black, in bold figures: the same taste as the script, which is made of thick strokes of high contrast. The blue-gray robe of a water judge looks like slate out of doors.
- **People help the light.** Walls that face the day are washed white. Cairns carry reflective tops. The rail of the Whisper Road shows as a bright line across the country.
- **Birni.** The city rises in terraces from the nightward shore of the Bahari, so it faces the sun across 45 kilometers of water. On a clear day a road of red light lies on the lake from the far shore to the foot of the city.

## Light Indoors

- Rooms are dim. Windows are small and high, on the dayward wall.
- A house has three lights, and each has its color. **Glowcap jars** give a soft blue-green. **Oil lamps** give yellow, and the oil is measured. **Power** from the wind banks goes first to pumps, growing towers, rail and public works; a house has a small share by quota, enough for a hot plate or one lamp at set bells.
- A face looks different in each: warm and red in the doorway, green beside the jar, yellow over the lamp.
- Darkness is not given on the surface. People make it. See Sleep.

# Wind

## On the Body

- The wind at the ground comes from the night side, always. On the floor of the rift it is about 20 kilometers an hour. On open ground it is 30 to 40. The north is the windiest region.
- At 20, cloth and hair move, dust walks along the ground, and a hat stays on. At 40, a person leans to walk nightward, speaks loud, and holds on to anything light.
- People who grow up in it lean on it and do not know that they lean. When they step into a lee they fall a half-step forward. Country people do this at every city gate, and city children laugh.
- **The stance is side-on:** one shoulder to the wind, feet apart. Two equals who stop to talk in the open stand face to face along the belt, each with a shoulder to the night.
- **One cheek is cold.** On a path along the belt the nightward cheek loses its feeling, and the eye on that side runs. Ears ache, so the scarf covers them.
- **The back is the cold part of a person who sits,** because every seat faces the day. Coats are thickest across the back, and collars are high.

## Sound

- Out of doors it is never silent. The wind is a hum under every other sound.
- Each place has its own note. Roofs hum under their nets of cable. Towers give a low note that a person feels in the back teeth. Turbines beat. Storm chimes, wind-flutes, flags and kites add to it. A person who comes home knows the town by ear before they see it.
- Buildings creak and groan when the pressure shifts. People who live in a house know its sounds and wake when one of them is wrong.
- **There are two voices.** The outdoor voice is loud and short, and the hands help it. The indoor voice is low. A person who brings the outdoor voice into a room is told so.
- **Sound goes dayward.** A bell is heard far down the wind and hardly at all up it. The bell house and the drum stand at the nightward end of a town.
- In high wind people use the storm register and say numbers twice.

## Lee and Stillness

These are two different things, and people feel them in opposite ways.

- **Lee** (*kinga*) is shelter. The wind is still heard overhead, and here it does not reach. A court, a wall, the back of a friend. It is the Duskaran idea of comfort.
- **Stillness** is when the wind itself stops. It is rare. Flags hang, the roofs go quiet, and the animals stop. People stop too, and look nightward. Still air means that cold is collecting on the ground, or that something is holding a line. Children are sent indoors.

## What the Wind Does to Habits

- Nothing light is left loose. Every object has a cord, a peg, a net or a stone on it. Children tie good knots early.
- Paper and flame are for indoors. Out of doors people use slate, a tally board and knots in a cord.
- Water is poured low, in the lee, with the spout on the rim of the cup.
- Dust is swept out of the dayward door, with the wind.
- Smoke lies flat as soon as it leaves the vent and goes dayward. Fires stand on the dayward side of a court.
- A kite flies with no skill at all. It is a child's first toy. Houses fly a family kite at the Storm Wall Festival.
- Seeds and spores travel on the wind. In some phases the winged seeds of the windvine cross a town like a slow rain, and people sneeze.

# Air

## Smell

**A person smells only what is nightward of them.** The wind does not turn, so it never brings a smell from the day side. A town smells the country nightward of it and does not smell itself.

- The base smell of open air is the smell of the night: ice, wet stone, cold.
- Every town has a **clean end** and a **used end.** At the nightward end are the wall, the belt of stormroot, the turbines, the condensers, which face the damp air as it arrives, and the cisterns. At the dayward end are the goat pens, the recyclers, the drying yards, the tanners and the forges, and the greenhouses, which need the light.
- People who live at the dayward end live downwind. It is said plainly and without shame, but everybody knows who does.

| Place | Smell |
|-------|-------|
| Open ground, nightward of a town | Cold stone and ice; wet earth after a line |
| The stormroot belt | Resin and damp bark |
| The lanes | Dust, hearth smoke, lamp oil, the sour of fermenting porridge |
| A court | Goat hair, oiled wool, flour, the porridge crock |
| A growing tower | Wet roots, green leaf, the pond smell of the feed water |
| A cistern | Cold, wet stone |
| A river or lake quarter | Mud, fish, wet rope |
| The dayward end | Goats, drying fish, the recyclers, hot metal |
| Near a vent | Sulfur |
| Before a storm | Copper on the tongue and a sharp smell of charge; sulfur with it before the worst ones |
| After rain | Wet dust |

- **Wet dust is the best-loved smell on Duskara.** It is the smell of the Water's Blessing Festival. Old people stand in a doorway for it.
- **A smell from the day side is an alarm.** Scorched rock and dry dust mean that hot air has come in against the wind, as it does at the hot gates. Everybody looks up.
- **People** smell of smoke, wool and skin oil. A person who has come off the road smells of dust, and one who has come from the steam house smells of resin, from the stormroot beams warm in the steam.

## Taste

- **Water has a home taste.** Melt water is flat and very cold. Cistern water tastes of stone. Condenser water tastes of nothing, and people put a grain of salt or a leaf in it. The springs of the Fire Land taste of the rock. A traveler knows the water of home with the first mouthful.
- "How is your water?" is a real question. The proper answer is that it is sweet.
- ***Tamu*, sweet, means good water before it means anything else.** The sweetness of sugar is rare: dried fruit, at feasts.
- New water upsets the belly. This is one reason for the boiled cup of welcome.
- The mouth is dry. Lips crack and people oil them. There is grit between the teeth after any walk on open ground.

## Warm and Cold

- The belt runs from -5 degrees at the nightward limit to +40 at the dayward limit. A heartland town in the nightward half has about 12 to 18 degrees, with wind. People wear a coat and a scarf for all of their lives.
- **Temperature belongs to places, not to times.** There is no cold hour and no warm one. A person knows the warm corner of the court, the cold step of the stair, and the turn of the path where the scarf goes on.
- So a change in a known place is noticed at once, and it worries people.
- Cold lies in hollows and against the dark face of a wall. Warmth stays on lit walls and near the ground of the Fire Land.
- The wind is dry in most phases. Mist stands near the ice, the springs and the lake.

# Water in the Hand

Water is the most precious thing, and waste is the worst fault. The rivers pass through and the wind takes what stands open, so a settlement catches water, cleans it, uses it and gives it back. A Resource Allocation Committee sets the rations, and water tokens belong to the people who have authority over the valves. In a kitchen, all of this comes down to a measure, two jars and a tally.

## The Measure

- Each person has a **measure** (*kibaba*) of sweet water for one day. In the heartland it is about ten liters: one jar, which a child of a hundred Cycles can carry. It is smaller in the Dry Land and larger in the Ice Land.
- The household draws its measures at the **water tally** (*ubao-maji*) of the quarter, and the keeper writes each one.
- A house has **two jars.** The sweet jar is sealed, and it stands against the nightward wall, where it is cool. The used jar takes all water that has served once (*maji-pili*, second water). It goes back to the hydroloop or to the plants. No water is thrown on the ground.

## The Stair of Uses

Water goes down this stair and never up it.

1. Drinking and cooking.
2. Hands.
3. The body.
4. Cloth.
5. The floor and the plants.

## Washing

- **Hands.** One person pours from a small jug over the hands of another, above a basin, for about three breaths. The basin goes to the used jar. This is done before every meal, and the youngest person at the table pours.
- **The body.** A cloth and one cup, each day. Oil and a scraper take off the dust.
- **Teeth.** A chewing stick (*mswaki*). No water.
- **Soap** (*sabuni*) is soft and brown, and it smells of smoke. A household boils goat tallow with the lye of hearth ash: the ash of *kuni* makes good lye, and the surface has no wood ash. Soap needs rinse water, so people use it where dirt can kill and not where it is only dust: the hands of the healer, the birth room, the hands of the recycler crew, the cook after meat. A pot of it stands in each steam house.
- **Pots and bowls** are scoured dry, with hearth ash and fine sand, and then wiped. The bowl is already empty. A little second water finishes the work.
- **To spit is to waste.** A person who must spit does it into the latrine or into a cloth.
- **The steam house** (*hamamu*). In the north and in the caves steam costs nothing, and the Fire Land has baths. A heartland town has a steam house in each quarter, heated by the thermal exchanger. Its beams are stormroot, and in the steam they give off their resin. The steam room is a dome about 12 meters across, sunk a little below the lane, with benches in two tiers round its wall. The exchanger heats a boiler in a room against the back wall, and warm pipes run under the floor. Steam turns to water on the cold dome and runs down to gutters at its foot and back to the hydroloop, so the steam house loses little water. On the dayward side are the undressing room, the oil room and a cool room, and the door opens on steps down from the lane, in the lee. A person goes once in a few days. It is the place where news passes.
- **Hair** is braided close to the head or wrapped in a scarf. This keeps the dust out and needs little water. Braiding is done in the court, in the lee, and it takes hours of talk.
- **Clothes** are beaten, aired in the wind and hung on the lit wall. They are seldom washed, and wool almost never.
- **The latrine** is dry. What it holds goes to the recyclers.
- **The Deepkin** have steam and warm water without cost, and they wash every day. A Belter in the caves finds that nobody there smells of skin oil, and feels dirty for the first time.

## Drinking and Pouring

- Water from the river is never drunk raw. It comes to the house from the hydroloop.
- People drink little plain water. Most of the fluid of a day is thin porridge, chai and light grain ferment.
- The cup is small. A host fills it to two thirds, because a full cup spills.
- To spill is a fault. To spill and not see it is a worse one. When water is spilled the room is quiet for one breath, and nobody makes a joke until the person who spilled it does.
- The first test of a small child is to carry a full cup across the court in the wind.

## The Sound of Water

- People know the sound of their pipes. A drip wakes a sleeper.
- Anyone may knock on any door, at any bell, to report a joint that drips.
- After a line of storm the gutters run into the cisterns. People stop work to listen to it.

## Gifts and Secrets

- ***Sadaka*** is a gift of water with no witness. A sealed jar stands at a door when the house wakes. Nobody asks who put it there.
- **The old jar.** The Thirst Wars were about twenty years ago. Many people who are old enough to remember the embargo keep one sealed jar more than their measure. By the letter of the law this is hoarding, and the word for it is *kinabara*. Their families know and do not speak of it.

# The Day

## The Bells

A day has ten bells: five in the waking period and five in the rest period. The first bell of the day is *sori*, and the rest period begins at *kiza*.

- The **five day bells** (*kengele-kuu*) are loud. At each one many people stop for one breath and one word of thanks.
- The **five rest bells** (*kengele-pole*) are one soft stroke each. They are for the watch.
- A bell is also a length of time: "for the rest of the bell". It is a little less than two and a half hours.
- Duskara has clocks and watches (*saa*). The bell house keeps a clock, its keeper rings by it, and many people carry a watch. Hours and minutes serve for short things. The bell stays the measure of the shared day: a watch tells one person the hour, and a bell tells everybody at once.
- A day is ten bells. It is as long as a day of Earth, near enough: a Cycle has not quite thirty-five of them.

| Bell | Hours after *sori* | What it marks |
|------|-------------------:|---------------|
| First of the day (*sori*) | 0 | Work begins. It is not the bell of waking: people are up before it. |
| Second | about 2.5 | Markets open. Lessons begin. |
| Third | about 5 | The middle of the work. Shifts change. |
| Fourth | about 7 | The meal, which lasts about an hour and a half. |
| Fifth, the last bell | about 9.5 | The public day ends. Halls close and children go down. In the Cycle of *saumu*, people eat after it. |
| First of the rest (*kiza*) | 12 | First sleep. |
| Second of the rest | about 14.5 | |
| Third of the rest | about 17 | The middle wake. |
| Fourth of the rest | about 19 | Second sleep. |
| Fifth of the rest | about 21.5 | Rising: the cloth and the cup, thin porridge, chai, the walk to work. |

The old words survive for the parts of the waking period: morning from the first bell to the second, midday to the third, afternoon to the fourth, evening from the fourth to *kiza*.

## Sleep

- **Darkness is made.** The sleeping room is the lowest and darkest room of the house, cut into the ground, where it is also warm and quiet. People say that they "go down" for sleep and "come up" for waking. Where the rock does not allow a lower room, sleeping rooms have shutters.
- On the road a sleeper ties a cloth over the eyes (*nguo-lala*), and a caravan carries a bell.
- **Many people sleep twice.** The first sleep is four or five hours. Then comes the **middle wake** (*kesha*), at about the third rest bell: a quiet hour of low talk, of partners, of mending, of a word to the wind, of a look at the child. Then the second sleep. Other people sleep through, and nobody remarks on either habit.
- A town never sleeps all at once. The watch and the rest shift keep the works.
- A child has no dark outside to tell it that the day is over. It learns to sleep by the bell, and a child who "will not go down" is the common trouble of parents.
- **Out of bell** (*nje-kengele*). A person whose sleep has left the bells is in some trouble: grief, sickness, or a new ability. Neighbors see the lamp and hear the steps. It is often the first thing that a witness sees.
- A watch tells the hour and does not make a person sleepy. A person alone, with no bell and no company, drifts: the body makes a longer day. A lone traveler comes into a town "a bell late" and needs two days to come back to the count.

## Rest Days

By old custom a person rests one day in seven, as the caravans do. The rota sets the day for each person, so a town never stops all at once. The span of seven days has no name.

## Longer Time

- No part of the Cycle is warm and no part is cold. The weather has **phases**: phases of storm and of calm, of cold wind and of warm wind. Over about seven years the star's breath brings the Long Dry and then the rains again. People feel them in the lips, the joints and their sleep.
- A farming town counts the Cycle by its grain. People say "at this point of the Cycle" of a crop and of a river.
- People date their own lives by storms, by the heading of the grain, by festivals and by births. The count of Cycles is for contracts and records.
- A child's age is given in Cycles and an adult's in years.

# The House and the Lane

## The Common House

This pattern follows from the wind and the light. Rich houses and city blocks change it, but they keep its order.

- **The back** is the nightward wall: thick, low, with no opening. The wind and the rain strike it. The gutter and the cistern are at its foot, and the sweet jar stands against it inside. A weatherworking house also turns its back to the night.
- **The roof** is low at the back and rises toward the day. Nets of cable or rows of stones hold it down, and it hums.
- **The face** is the dayward wall: the door, the high windows, and a bench. It is lit, and it is in the lee.
- **The court** lies on the dayward side. It holds the hearth (*jiko*), the porridge crock, the loom, the drying lines, and in small places the goats. The houses of one line share a court: this is the compound.
- **Below** are the store and the sleeping room.
- **Inside**: mats and low stools. A shelf with the cups of the family, each one different. In many houses, most of all in the north, a shelf of name tablets. Wrap cloths with their sayings, hung as curtains. Tools on pegs, each one carved.
- **The cistern** at the foot of the back wall holds the rain of the roof. It is for the lower stairs of water: cloth, the floor and the plants. Nobody drinks it, and it is outside the measure, but the keeper of the tally reads its level after each storm.
- **The heat stone**, a geothermal crystal, lies in a lined bin under the sleeping room.
- The used water goes by a pipe under the court to the hydroloop line in the long lane. The power line comes in under the roof and feeds the hot plate.
- The dry latrine stands at the dayward side of the court, downwind. The stair goes down along the day face.
- **Sizes.** A common house is about 7 meters deep. The lower rooms are about 2.5 meters below the ground. The roof rises from about 2.2 meters at the back to 4.7 at the face, and the court is about 7 meters deep.

## The House Stays

A house belongs to a line and stays in it. People are born and die in the same compound, and a house holds the cups, the tablets and the carvings of many generations. A Belter who hears that the Deepkin change rooms by rota does not know what to say.

## Marks of the Kin

A person has three kin: the **house**, which is the compound of one line; the **line** (*ukoo*), which gives the line name; and the **Wind-Kin**, one of five clans that group the lines along the whole arc. Each Wind-Kin has a sign, carved with a few cuts.

| Wind-Kin | The sign | What it shows |
|----------|----------|---------------|
| *kin-Hanga*, Wind | Three strokes that lean the same way | Grass in the wind |
| *kin-Moto*, Fire | An arc over a short bar | The hearth |
| *kin-Maji*, Water | Three steps going down, a dot on the top one | The stair of uses |
| *kin-Babu*, Elder | A cord with a knot and a loop | The knotted cord of the tally |
| *kin-Kivuli*, Shadow | An arch under a ground line | The mouth of a cave |

- **The house mark** is the sign of the Wind-Kin. The house draws its measures under it at the tally.
- The sign is carved on the **doorpost** and under the **cups** of the family, on the **name tablets** of the dead and on the **seals** of contracts. It is **worn** as a small pendant of carved wood on a cord, or stitched in a light thread into the sash of adulthood. A seal is pressed in red clay.
- A person with no Wind-Kin has no sign, and draws water under the line name.

## Fuel

The surface has little to burn. The only wood is stormroot, and a council releases it for beams and drums, not for fire.

- **The hearth burns cake** (*kuni*): bricks pressed from grain straw, the waste of sunspire stalks and goat dung, and dried on the lit wall. It burns slowly, with a sour smoke. This is the smoke of the lanes.
- **The collective kitchens and the kiln of the dead burn gas** from the recyclers.
- **A hot plate** runs on the power quota of the house.
- **Much heat is not fire:** the thermal exchangers, the geothermal crystals that give back stored heat for a whole phase, and the vents of the Fire Land, which need no fuel.
- **Lamp oil** is pressed from the seed crops.
- A flame is small and watched. A large open fire is a feast or it is waste.

## The Latrine and the Recycler

- **The latrine is dry.** It has two jars: one for urine and one for the rest. After use, a person throws a scoop of hearth ash or dry dust into the second jar. Ash closes the smell, and the house always has ash.
- **The yellow jar** stands for some days. Then it goes, mixed with second water, to the growing towers and the plants of the court. It is the last step of the stair of uses.
- **The solid jar** goes to the recyclers. A crew on the rota carries the jars down to the dayward end on a covered cart, once in a few days. A water judge takes this turn too.
- **At the recycler** the waste goes into sealed, warm tanks, heated by the thermal exchanger. The tanks give gas for the kitchens and the kiln, and a wet residue. The water of the residue goes to the stills and back into the hydroloop. What stays is cured for a Cycle and becomes compost for the growing towers.
- People know where some of their water has been. They do not speak of it at the table.
- **A latrine that leaks into the ground** fouls the soil, and it is the fault of the land-poisoner (*tangazali*). The penalty is the work of cleaning.
- The recycler crew washes with soap at the end of the turn and goes to the steam house. A person who comes off that turn smells of resin, like any other bather.

## The Door

- A visitor stops outside and calls *hujambo*. The house answers *karibu*.
- Shoes come off. A hand brush hangs by the door, and the visitor brushes the dust from coat and hair before they step in.
- The cup comes before any business.
- A cloth tied to the ring of a door means that the house wants to be left alone, and neighbors respect it.

## The Lanes

- **Cross lanes** run from the night to the day. The wind and the light go down them together. Builders make them so, to lead the wind through a city. They are bright and loud, and they hold the turbines and the carts.
- **Long lanes** run along the belt. They are calm and dim. The doors, the stalls and the children are there.
- Markets stand in courts that the wind does not reach.
- A town climbs in terraces where it can, so that each row sees over the next toward the day.
- Carvings cover the windward walls. At the height of a hand they are worn smooth.
- **What a lane sounds like:** the hum, the bells, the drum, goats, a loom, children who say a survival poem in chorus, and from the cricket sheds a steady song. Some children keep one cricket in a small cage of fiber, for its voice.

## The Shape of a Town

A town of the heartland is about 680 meters across the belt and 490 meters along it.

- **The clean end**, from the night: the wall, open at each cross lane so that the wind goes down the lanes; the stormroot belt inside it; the turbines; the condensers; the cisterns; the bell house with the drum.
- **The rows** of compounds stand on terraces about 1.5 meters high. A block holds five compounds between two cross lanes. Cross lanes are about 80 meters apart and about 8 meters wide; long lanes are about 2 meters wide.
- **A quarter** is the ground around one main cross lane, in the middle of the town: the hall, the market court in its lee, the water tally, the steam house, the healing house and a growing tower. A heartland town has two quarters, and the council floor stands in one of them.
- **The hydroloop** has two lines under the main cross lanes: sweet water from the cisterns, and used water down to the recyclers.
- **The used end**, toward the day: the Whisper Road on its embankment, with the halt and the Thread on poles; the track of the caravans; the guest house by the halt; the goat pens, the recyclers, the drying yards, the forges and the greenhouses; the drying house, with the kiln of the dead beside it. The fields lie beyond, and the far house stands in them.

# Food

A meal on the belt is counted, shared and finished.

## Where Food Grows

- **Grain grows in strips.** Wind-grain is short and stiff in the stalk. It grows in long strips that lie along the belt, each in the lee of a line of stormroot or of a low wall set across the wind. Beyond the strip stands the next line, and beyond it the next strip.
- The wind never changes its side, so a line gives the same shelter every day: a strip as deep as ten to fifteen times the height of the line. Open ground with no line grows grass, not grain.
- From the road the grain land looks open: pale strips of grain, and dark lines of trees across the wind.
- A town is a windbreak too. Its fields lie at its dayward end, in its lee.
- Leaf, herbs, roots and a little grain grow in the growing towers of the quarters. Beans and the seed crops grow in the greenhouses at the day end.
- **The growing tower** (*shamba-juu*) is round, about 8 meters across and 24 high. Its nightward half is stone without openings; its dayward half is glass. A wall that faces the sun takes the low light square on, where a field takes it at a slant: this is why the farms of a town stand up. A band of carving runs at the foot of the stone half: grain, water, the balance.
- **Its floors**, from the top: green leaf, which needs the most sun; herbs and chai leaf; roots in deep troughs; dwarf wheat; beans on poles; and the nursery under the lamps. The lower floors are in the shadow of the rows in front and rely more on the lamps.
- **The lamps** are blue-white. They give the blue that the red sun lacks, and from outside the glass shows a cold blue light, seen nowhere else in a town.
- **Its water** runs from a tank on the roof down through the beds by its own weight to a sump in the cellar, and a wind pump on the roof lifts it back. Gutters at the foot of the glass take the water that the leaves breathe out. The feed room takes in the second water and the water of the yellow jar, and compost from the recyclers steeps in a vat there: this is the pond smell of the feed water. Glowcaps grow on shelves in the cellar beside the sump, and warm water from the exchanger of the steam house keeps the cellar and the core warm.
- The door is in the lee, with a tally slate. Leaf goes out by the tally of the quarter.
- **The growing thread.** A keeper who thinks a row is slow ties a red thread round one stalk, under the top joint, and reads the next days of growth against it.

## Through the Day

| When | What | Where |
|------|------|-------|
| Before the first bell | Thin sour porridge (*uji*), warm, and chai | At the hearth of the house |
| During work | Flatbread in a cloth, bean paste, dried fruit, roasted pods of windvine | Where the work is |
| The fourth bell | Sima, stew, pickle, a little fish or meat | The hall of the compound or the quarter |
| After the meal | Light ferment; wind-wine at feasts; stories | The hall |

## At the Table

- Hands are washed by pouring. The water-blessing comes before the food.
- **The right hand eats.** A person pinches off a piece of sima, rolls it, presses a hollow in it with the thumb, and takes up the stew with it.
- **The cook serves and the cook counts.** Nobody takes for themselves.
- **Nobody leaves food in a bowl.** A person who cannot finish hands the bowl to a neighbor, who empties it and says nothing about it.
- The collective kitchen cooks once for many people and saves fuel. The hearth of a house makes chai and porridge and warms what was cooked elsewhere.

## Taste

- **Sour comes first.** Fermented food keeps with no fuel and no cold. A Duskaran child knows sour before it knows any other taste, because the first food is the thin porridge.
- Then salt, from the salt flats of the Dayward Edge, then smoke, then the bitter leaf of the herb teas.
- **Fresh green leaf** from the growing towers is the small luxury of an ordinary day. Fresh fruit is rare.
- Feast food is rice, goat, fresh fish and wind-wine.
- Food is enough and seldom more. Portions follow the work, and a person who has done a hard shift is served first.

## By Region

- The north and the caves steam their food, because steam is free there. Some old crew lines of the north still eat with two sticks at feasts.
- The cities of the lake eat fresh fish. Rice grows only at Mdomo-Luŋga, and it stays a feast food.
- The Fire Land cooks on the vents.
- Fried crickets are sold in the markets of poor quarters and of the Dry Land, in a folded leaf.

# The Body and Its Cover

## Clothes

- Layers that can be opened and closed: a wrap cloth, a coat, trousers, boots, a scarf.
- Wind-wear (*hanga-vazi*) is for open ground: tight cuffs, tied ankles, a hood.
- Fiber comes from the stalk of the sunspire and from the hair of the goats that every town keeps. The shed fur of the ember stalker goes into coats for the cold.
- **The wrap cloth** (*kanga*) **carries a saying.** The saying is chosen with care. A cloth that is given as a gift says what the giver will not say aloud, and the person who receives it reads it twice.
- **Marks that others read:** the sash of adulthood, the ash of a calling, a bond-token, the blue-gray of a water judge.

## The Body

- Brown skin in many shades, and hair of every kind. A Belter has a reflective layer in the eye, and in a dim room it can catch the lamp.
- **Loads go on the back,** with a band across the shoulders or on a frame (*kiunzi*). A person who walks uphill with empty hands, when something has to go up, hears about it.
- **Hands are not idle.** A person who sits carves, spins or knots. This is why every tool, cup and doorpost is carved, and why a child has a small carved thing in a pocket.
- **People touch.** A hand on the back, on the neck, on the face. Any adult of the compound picks up any child.
- **People point with the chin or the lips.** A finger that points at a person is an accusation.
- **What is in a pocket:** a knife, a length of cord, a chewing stick, a carved thing, and at council time a wind-token.

## Gender

- **The language does not sort people.** Creole has one pronoun for a third person, *ŋa*, and a personal name fits anyone. Only a few kin words carry a gender: *ama*, *bapa*, *bibi*.
- **Work does not sort them either.** The rota looks at skill and at who is free. Clothes, hair and seats on a council are the same for all.
- **Gender is still there.** People have one, most say it with a kin word or not at all, and a body matters to the healer and in a pregnancy. Some settlements have named roles for people who are neither or both, and each has its own words for them.
- **Care.** A healer (*tiba-ji*) treats the alignment of a body with the gender of the person as ordinary care. People speak of it as they speak of any cure: the person is coming to *sawa*.
- **What changes in the house** is one word. The family begins to say *bapa* where it said *ama*, or the reverse, and by custom the youngest says it first, at the meal.
- A stranger does not ask, and does not guess aloud.

## The Blood

- **The drug changes it.** Most adults take the contraceptive drug (*dawa-zuia*), and to take it is the normal state. In most people who would bleed, it stops the bleeding or makes it small. So the blood is a thing of the young, of a person who has stopped the drug for a child, and of the few who do not take it.
- **The first blood** comes near the age of the Rite of the Winds. It has no rite. The healer explains the drug, and an older person of the house shows the pad.
- **The pad** is a fold of carded sunspire pith in a cloth. It is not washed: it goes into the latrine jar, and so to the recyclers. People say that the body gives its water back, as they say of the dead, and they say it without weight.
- **Nothing is closed to a person who bleeds.** The steam house, the kitchen, the council and the guild house are the same for all. No custom calls the blood unclean.
- **Pain** is treated like any small ill: a warm cloth on the belly, an herb tea, a bell in the lower room. A person may trade a turn on the rota, and by custom nobody asks why a turn is traded.

## Small Ills

- Cracked lips and knuckles. Eyes that run. Ears that ache. A dry cough in dust phases. One stiff shoulder, on the side that takes the wind on the road to work.
- Chilblains toward the night and heat sickness toward the day.
- Thermal sensors have migraines. Weatherworkers have nosebleeds and a shake in the hands.
- The healer is the *tiba-ji* or the *dok*. The common cures are herb teas and fungal salves, frostbloom tea for the cold, the sap of the warmedge cactus on a wound, and glowcap for the chest.

# Sickness

## The Healer

- The healer (*tiba-ji*, *dok*) has a trade like any other, and takes turns on the rota like any other person.
- A heartland town has a **healing house** (*tiba-wan*) in each quarter, near the steam house. It stands just down-belt of the steam house: a room of about 8 by 6 meters with its back to the night and its door in the lee. It has three mats, a still for clean water, a pot of soap and the shelf of remedies: herb teas, glowcap preparations, salves of seed oil and ash.
- Healers keep a guild (*chama*) across the towns of a region. Its grades and its examination follow the pattern of the weatherworking guilds.
- A healer who is also a thermal sensor reads a body by the palm: fever, a hidden wound, a cold limb. People call such a healer "a warm hand".
- **Counting as care.** The healer reads the water tally every day. A house that draws less water, or takes fewer bowls from the common kitchen, is often a house with a sick person in it. The tally sees sickness before the healer does.

## Catching Sickness

- People know that some ills go from one person to another: the cough that goes through a caravan, the belly sickness of bad water, the fever that comes with new travelers. Most of them come by the roads.
- **The wind carries it dayward.** People believe this, and it is often true. So a person with a catching sickness lies at the **far house** (*mbali-wan*). It stands in the fields beyond the used end, about 25 meters past the last works, so that nothing of the town is downwind of it. It has three mats and the ochre cloth on its door.
- A sick house in the town has a strip of ochre cloth on the ring of its door. It is not the undyed cloth of mourning, and nobody confuses the two. Neighbors leave cooked food and sealed water on the step, knock twice and step back.
- **The measure of the sick** stays full. A fever takes water, and the healer may ask the committee for more. This is never called hoarding.
- **To hide a fever is to lie.** It is the same fault as to lie about health before two people first lie together. It covers a traveler who hides a cough at the gate, and the council hears it as it hears waste.
- **A dead person of a catching sickness** has the vigil with the door open, but nobody goes down. People speak the name from the step. The drying and the kiln are as usual. From the far house the keepers carry the body to the drying house along the used end, not through the lanes.

## At the Gate

- The boiled cup of welcome and the three days of a guest are also a watch. The guest house stands by the halt, where travelers come in from the road and the caravan track. If a guest has fever in the three days, the guest house becomes a sick house.
- Where the town has a thermal sensor at the gate, each traveler gives a palm on arrival. The sensor stands at the halt. "To give the palm" is ordinary courtesy, and a traveler who refuses it is watched.

## A Closed Town

- When a catching sickness spreads, the council can close the gate. The healer gives the count, and the council decides in the open, as it decides everything else.
- A closed town sends the warning down the relays and the wind songs, and the Caravan Guilds carry it along the routes.
- Neighbors bring loads to a **drop stone** outside the wall at the clean end, about 100 meters from it, on the line of a main cross lane. The carriers stay upwind of the town. They put the load down, step back, and wait until the people of the town take it. This is mutual aid under the Accord, and the debt is remembered on both sides.
- Trade stops, and every day of a closed gate is counted. So councils close late, and they are blamed either way.

# Manners

The Creole word is *adabu*.

## Meeting and Leaving

- In the heartland a greeting is short. On the caravan roads it is long: family, water, road, and then the business.
- The farewell is *hanga-salama*, safe winds.
- An elder is addressed as *bapa* or *ama*.
- At each day bell a person may stop in the middle of a sentence for one breath. The other person waits and does not take it as a slight.
- A person who prays turns to face the wind. So prayer is done with the back to the light and the face in the cold.

## In the Wind

- **Give your lee.** A person who stops in the open with an elder, a child or someone under a load steps to their nightward side and takes the wind on the back.
- **Do not stand in another person's wind.** To block the wind of a drying line, a hearth or a kite is the small form of a great offense, and the answer is "you are in my wind".
- A person who must pass close goes by on the dayward side, so that their dust does not fall on the other.

## About Water and Food

- Pour low. Close the jar. Empty the bowl.
- Take the cup that is offered.
- Never say aloud how much water another person holds, and never ask.

## About Counting

- To say aloud what a person has carried is polite, and it is expected.
- To say aloud what a person failed to carry is an accusation, and it belongs before a council.

## Speech

- A careful speaker says how they know a thing: seen, heard or sensed. People do it in gossip too. News without one of these words is taken as seen, so a person who passes on a rumor as plain fact has lied twice.
- In council a person asks permission before they speak. Things are talked out until the group agrees, and this is slow. People bring their carving.
- The great insults are about waste. They are almost never spoken: people use a roundabout phrase or a hand sign.
- **Humor** lives on counting, on loads and on the wind. People tease the ones they like. A stranger who is teased has been accepted.

## Privacy

- People are seldom alone. To work alone is the thing that every guild fears, and the feeling reaches beyond the guilds: a person who keeps apart for long is watched with some care.
- The answer to a crowd is not a wall. It is to stand side-on and let it go past.
- Neighbors do not see what a tied door hides. They do see a person who has gone out of bell.
- A person with an ability is always partly elsewhere. Nobody asks them what the air is doing while they eat.

## At a Storm Haven

- Nobody is turned away from a haven. A quarrel waits at the door.
- Take what you need, leave what you can, and write both on the slate of the haven.
- The first in makes chai for those who come after.
- The seals of the water jars are counted, and a jar is opened only in a storm.
- In the dark of a storm someone rings the bells of the haven and keeps the count, so that nobody comes out "a bell late".
- After a great storm the nearest town sends a team to check its havens and to look for the people whom the storm did not give back.

## What a Stranger Notices

A Deepkin on the first day above sees these small acts before anything else. Everybody does them, and nobody thinks about them.

1. People turn a shoulder to the wind before they speak.
2. They look nightward as they come out of a door.
3. They stop for one breath at a bell.
4. Shoes come off at the door, and dust is brushed from coat and hair.
5. A cup is offered, and it is taken.
6. Water is poured with the spout on the rim.
7. What is put down is tied down.
8. Sima is rolled and pressed with the thumb.
9. A load is counted aloud at a door.
10. News begins with "seen" or "heard".

# Work

- Every able person gives time to the works of the settlement: turbines, cisterns, routes. A water judge takes a turn on the cistern crew like any other person.
- The **rota** (*zamu*) sets the turns. A shift is about two bells. Most people work one shift at their trade and give part of another to the household or the rota.
- **Everything is counted**: loads, bowls, empty sacks, rows of grain, measures of water. There is a slate or a tally board at every door where goods pass.
- Children scrape the scale from the condensers every seven days. It is their first work for the town, and they are proud of it.
- The market is open from the second bell to the third. Much of the trade is by hand sign, and a deal closes on *nzuri*.
- A person is paid in rations and in Windmarks, which stand for water, work and power.
- **Nothing goes up empty.** Where a guild house or a watch stands above its town, each person who climbs to it carries part of its supplies, whatever the errand. The loads wait at the foot of the path with a slate, and the climber writes their name against the load they take. The cook of the house weighs each load at the door and says it aloud. Empties go down the same way, with whoever leaves, and are counted out.
- **The flood ring.** River fishers paint a ring of tar round each weir post at the height of the water, so that the next rise or fall can be read against it.
- **Marker stones.** An ice harvester who begins to cut sets a stone a measured number of paces out from the ice front. The family reads the advance or the retreat of the ice against the stones of the generations before.

## What Every Child Can Do

By about ninety Cycles, when lessons begin, a child can:

- tie down a load so that it stays;
- pour without loss;
- read the nightward sky for a line that is coming;
- make the hand signals and speak the storm register;
- find the nearest storm haven from any place in the town;
- say the survival poems of the house;
- keep a tally.

## Far Work

A town that owes what it cannot pay in water or goods pays in work, and a town that lacks a skill borrows it. This is *kazi-mbali*, far work. It has a rule, and it has a practice.

**The rule**

- **The roster.** It hangs at the hall, with the town, the work and the number of Cycles written at the head. A person writes their own name on it, before the kin leader or a Wayseer, and can strike it with the same hand until the day of departure.
- **The terms** are fixed with the home council before departure: the town, the work, how many Cycles, the measures, letters carried without charge, and the right to come home if the receiving town breaks the terms.
- **The Wayseer asks each person alone,** once, and writes the answer.
- **The worker keeps their place.** Their line, their Wind-Kin and their room in the house of their line wait for them. The rota of the home lane counts them as away, not as gone.
- **In the receiving town** the worker draws their measures at the tally like anyone else and lodges where the terms say: the guest house for a short work, a room in a house of the town for a long one.
- **Far work brings no birth authorization.** A worker who wants a child asks the council of the town where the child will be raised.

**The practice**

- A place on the roster is called an honor, and in a poor settlement it is often a price. A council that needs water or a weatherworker finds people who will "choose" to go, and they are most often people without an ability, whose work is easiest to lend.
- **The signs** are known: a roster where every name comes from the same end of the town; a name in a hand that is not the person's own; a family whose ration improved in the same Cycle.
- Here the witness has work. A Wayseer who doubts can refuse to take a name.

**The burden**

- The people who leave and the people who stay carry a grief, and it is called by the plain word for a load, *mzigo*. The lane takes the turns of the one who left for one Cycle.
- **Praise comes after, not before.** Nobody tells a person that it is brave to go until the name is on the roster by their own hand. Praise before the choice is pressure.
- **Many come back with somebody.** A worker who meets a partner in the other town trades the terms of the bond as anyone does, and the trade says who moves. Most marriages between towns begin this way.
- A letter between the ends of the arc and the Bahari is about four Cycles on the road by caravan, and about twenty days by Whisper Road.

## The Dead

The body is dried, so that its water returns to the settlement, and then it is burned.

1. **The vigil.** The body lies for one rest period in the lower room of its house. The kin oil it; they use no water. The door stands open for that rest period, against all other custom, and anyone may come down.
2. **The drying.** Keepers carry the body to the drying house, at the dayward end of the town. Its water goes back into the loop. People say that the dead person "has given back the water". The keeper of the tally strikes the name at that bell.
3. **The burning.** A kiln does it, on recycler gas, or on the heat of a vent in the Fire Land.
4. **The ash.** The kin carry it to a place where three currents meet. They face the wind, speak the name, and open the hand. The ash goes dayward.
5. **The tablet.** Houses that keep name tablets add one.
6. **The name.** Nobody in the close family carried it while the person lived. Now it is free, and the next child may have it.

- **Mourning.** The house ties an undyed cloth to the ring of its door for one Cycle. It does not light its hearth for seven days, and the neighbors bring cooked food. A parent who loses a child has leave from the rota.
- **A person whom a storm took and did not give back.** The kin speak the name and open an empty hand.
- **The Deepkin** have no wind. They burn their dead at a vent. The Bone Lorekeepers keep one small piece of burned bone from each of the dead in the wall of the Hearth, and they say the names.
- A bonded animal has its own rite.

- One breath and one word at each day bell.
- The water-blessing before food.
- A word to the wind, with the face in the cold.
- Wind-listening in company, often at *sori*.
- The warding gesture before a person goes into a storm.
- *Sadaka*.

## The Wall of a Wind Temple

- A Wind Temple keeps a wall that faces the wind, smoothed by hand. Each line of storm that the people of the place live through gets a row on it: the bell, and a short prayer spoken to the wind, as one speaks to a house. Names of the dead stand under some rows.
- The oldest rows are worn almost flat by the wind, and new rows are cut over them at the foot.
- A Watcher may cut an account of a working on the wall, in the same signs as the prayers.
- People pray with the face in the wind, at the wall, and the prayer is not written anywhere else.

## Where People Meet

## The Council Floor

- Any member of the settlement may speak in an open forum. A speaker asks permission and says how they know what they report.
- The things under discussion lie in the circle: a map, a tool, a token.
- In a forum a person votes with a smooth stone to keep things as they are, or with a carved wind-token to change them. A sitting council raises hands.
- A forum is long, because things are talked out. People bring their carving, and children sleep against the wall. A person who leaves before the end gives the reason aloud.
- A group of citizens can call an emergency audience, and the council must answer within three days.
- Workers petition against a guild and families against an allocation. A young person can appeal against their own parents.
- Seats turn with the wind phases, so most adults know somebody who has sat.
- **A guild house** holds its council in its hall, and votes as a forum does. The practitioners sit on a bench round a circle cut in the floor, and the head of the house sits on a stool at the nightward end. Each practitioner keeps a cloth bag with a river stone and a carved token, lays one of them by the thing in the circle, and takes it back after the count. The Held sit on a lower bench along the wall; they have no bag and no vote. The youngest of the ring pours the chai, and pours for the Held last.

## News

- The drum relay, the bell, the flute of a caravan, the Lorekeeper in the hall, the tally and the steam house.
- News from far towns comes down the Thread. The station posts it at the hall, and a runner carries what is urgent.
- A report carries its witness word. "Heard" news from the steam house is repeated as heard.

## Standing

- A person stands by what they carry. The rota board is public, so work is seen. Water is not seen: nobody says what another person holds.
- Lines that hold water rights and lines with an ability have weight. So has the end of the town where a family lives.
- Marks of standing are few: the sash, the robe of a judge, the numbered grade of a guild.
- The worst thing is to be named for waste. It is rare, and it goes before the council. The common penalty for a small fault is small and public: extra turns on the rota.
- There is no police. There are the safety teams, who are neighbors, the witness, and the Wayseer who mediates.
- A family that cannot live with its council moves to another town.

## Guests

- A stranger is *kinya* and a guest is *mgeni*. A traveler gets the cup, and then the keeper of the tally writes a guest measure.
- A person is a guest for three days. On the fourth the rota board carries their name.

## Together

- In a storm every person has a place in a storm haven (*hema-tufani*) and a task. The town drills for it, and after the storm the whole town repairs.
- When a house must be moved or built again, the lane lifts and carries without payment.
- At a feast the hall answers the storyteller, and nobody only watches.

# Courtship

Courtship is informal and personal. People meet at gatherings, on work details, or through a friend or a mentor. A person marries outside their own line. Consent is taught to everyone at the Threshold.

## Signs

- **Lee to an equal.** To give lee (*kinga*) to an elder is manners. To step to the nightward side of a person of your own age, who does not need it, is a declaration. If they stay in it, that is an answer.
- **The load.** To offer to take part of another person's load is a question. To be allowed is the first yes.
- **Counting.** A person who is interested remembers what the other carried, and says so.
- **The cloth.** A *kanga* with a chosen saying is the gift of courtship. To wear it is yes. To give it back folded is no, and nothing more is said.
- **A carved thing** for the pocket.
- **Hair.** To braid the hair of another person in the open court tells the whole lane.
- **The cup.** To pour for a person out of turn.
- **The dance.** Partners in the wind-dance often become partners in life.

## Rules

- **Ask in words, and ask once.** The opening is *Ni sema wazi ke?*, "may I speak openly?". A no is taken as it is given. A second ask is pressure. After a third, a friend of the other person comes to speak, as a witness does.
- **Never court with water.** Nor with food. A gift of either with a wish behind it is the first step of a crime. This is one reason why *sadaka* has no witness.
- **The first two questions are the line and the rota.** Two people of one line cannot marry, and two people on opposite shifts will seldom meet.
- **A person with an ability says so early.** People fear emotional weather, and a careful person with an ability keeps the first meetings in company.
- **Words.** *Mpenzi*, beloved, can bind as well as please. *Mahaba* is tenderness, and *upendo-huru* is love that is freely given.

## Places

- The lee of a wall on its lit side. The long lanes. The steps of the steam house. A roof, where there is more sun. The walk home from the hall after the fourth bell.
- Lovers in two towns write by caravan, ten to twelve days each way between neighbor cities.
- Far work cuts across all of this. A person who goes may leave somebody behind, and may find somebody where they go. See Work, Far Work.

# Married Life

## The Bond

- An everyday partnership needs no rite. Many couples mark it with the Wind's Embrace at a wind focal point.
- A formal partnership is *ndoa*. Its terms are traded first: this is *shanda*. They are written and witnessed when children or resources are part of it. The ceremony waits for a good wind, the partners exchange fans, and the feast is the *arusi*.
- **The trade covers:** which house; the measures of water; the rota; children, and the request for a birth authorization; what each owes to the kin of the other; if others are allowed; how it ends.
- **A marriage between two towns** is a public act, because one town loses a person and the other gains one. A Wayseer records the consent of each person. Nobody arranges it: the two have met on far work, on the caravan roads, at a festival, or in letters.

## The House

- A house belongs to a line and stays in it. So one partner moves, and the trade says which one.
- The one who moves keeps their line name and their Wind-Kin. A child takes the line of either parent and the clan of one.
- The one who moves comes into a compound: elders, cousins, a shared court. The couple has the lower room and the tied door.
- The house draws its measures together, under one mark at the tally.
- The two fans hang side by side on the wall. They carry the history of both lines.

## The Ordinary Day

- Partners on different shifts meet at the meal and in the middle wake (*kesha*).
- **The burden talk** is normal and is not a crisis: "I feel that the burden of the house is not balanced. Can we trade again?".
- **Renewal** (*shanda-upya*). A partnership is a choice that is made again. At each anniversary the partners sit before a few friends, say aloud what each one carries, and trade again. Each adds a mark to their fan.
- Many adults raise one child: a parent of a partner, a sibling, a friend of the house. The parent is the person who does the care.
- Partners ask for a birth together, and then they wait for the council. The wait is a known strain on a house.

## Trouble

- The compound hears everything. The first help is a witness.
- A partner can ask the kin leader or the council to review the bond. A Wayseer mediates, and a shared token can be held by the kin leader while they talk.
- People speak of a bond under strain as a vessel under pressure.

## The End

- A bond that is not renewed has ended, and the contract says how the goods and the care are divided.
- The partner who moved in goes back to the house of their own line, which has kept the place. Each fan goes back to its line.
- An end that was traded fairly carries no shame. Shame is for the person who left the other one carrying.

## Other Shapes

Three or four partners, with every term stated. Two friends who share a house and a child with no romance. Partners who do not want sex, and who join for work and care.

# Sex

Sex is a need like food and sleep. It carries no shame and no secrecy, and it rests on honesty and consent. Partners of the same sex have the same standing as any others.

## Custom

- **The three truths** (*kweli-tatu*). Before the first time, each person says three things: the state of their health, their agreements with other people, and their contraception. A lie in any of the three is a violation of consent, next to coercion.
- **Consent is spoken**, and it can be taken back at any breath.
- **Nobody gives water or food for it.**
- **A thermal sensor** reads fear and wanting in the heat of a skin. By custom such a person says that they can, once, at the start.
- To be exclusive is a term of the trade and is not assumed. People ask.

## Where and When

- The lower room, the middle wake, the tied door.
- **Quiet rooms.** A town or a city has rooms for people who have no privacy at home: a row of six small rooms on the down-belt side of the steam house. Nobody pays and nobody gives a name. A cloth on the ring shows that a room is in use.
- A small settlement has only timing and tact.

## The Body

- People go to the steam house first, if they can. Oil does the rest.
- The room is cold, so warmth is part of the pleasure. The word is *starehe*: comfort, pleasure and closeness in one.

## Birth Control

Duskara plans its growth, and the rules of birth follow from that.

- **Contraception is a drug** (*dawa-zuia*). The healer gives it to any adult who asks, and it does not count against a ration. To take it is the normal state, and it is taught at the Threshold.
- **Abortion** (*toa-mimba*) **is the choice of the mother.** She asks the healer. She needs no permission from a partner, a kin leader or a council, and nobody asks her for a reason.
- **A pregnancy without a birth authorization** (*ruhusa-zaa*) goes before the council.
  - The council can grant the authorization late, if the quota of the settlement has room.
  - The council can let the pregnancy go on without it. Then the household loses rations, and another household, one that holds an authorization, raises the child.
  - The council can order an abortion, if it is necessary.
- People know that this is hard. It is the rule that makes no exceptions, applied to the thing that people love most. A forced abortion is the gravest act that a council can order: it is rare, and nobody who sat on such a council forgets it.
- The child who is raised by another household is a full child of that house. It keeps no mark, and most towns do not tell.

## Talk

- People joke about it in the steam house, without malice and without names.
- Jealousy exists. It goes into the burden talk like any other load.

## In the Caves

Warm pools, a strong sense of privacy, and the dark. Fingers with glowing marks can say what the Deepkin do not say aloud.

# How It Feels

## The Ground Notes

- **Balance.** The highest praise for a person, a meal or a plan is *sawa*. A thing can be too generous or too bright as well as too mean or too dark.
- **To be counted is to be cared for.** The cook who counts the bowls will know who did not eat. A person whom nobody counts is a person whom nobody would miss. Children like to be counted, and lonely people stand near a tally.
- **Enough.** The good state is not plenty. It is a full cistern, a sealed jar and a crock that works. The sound of a full cistern is the sound of peace.
- **Carrying.** Pride is in what a person carries for others. "She carries" is a full compliment. The shame of the hoarder is the reverse of it.
- **Hidden kindness.** The best gift has no witness. So people are plain and even hard in what they say, and soft in what they do when nobody looks. A Duskaran who is thanked in public is uneasy.
- **The rule that makes no exceptions.** Everybody has seen it hurt somebody. It gives people a reserve: they do not promise what the measure may not allow, and they do not ask it of others.

## Comfort and Fear

| Comfort | Fear |
|---------|------|
| A lee, with the wind loud overhead | Still air |
| The roof that hums | A roof that goes quiet |
| The bells, and people at each one | A person out of bell; a lamp at the wrong hour |
| The hall at the fourth bell, when everybody talks | To work alone |
| The gutters that run after a line | The sound of a drip |
| Hot chai in cold hands | A smell from the day side |
| The steam house | To be lost: mist, a cave, a closed room |
| A known cold step and a known warm corner | A known place that has changed |

## The Two Sides

- **The sun is respected and not loved.** It is the Lion. It does not blink and it does not go away. The day side stands for force and for ambition that cannot be reached. Every seat faces the day, so people have the Lion before them for all of their lives, and they do not look at it: the eyes rest lower, on the court and on the work.
- **The night is where things come from.** Water, wind, and the dead who live in the wind. It is cold and it is not evil. A person who turns to pray turns toward it.
- **The two edges are always in view,** fire one way and ice the other, each within seven days on foot. People think of them as others think of a mountain above their town: seldom, and never without respect.

## Time

- Nothing in the world says that it is late. Only the bells say it, and the bells are other people. So time is company. A person who keeps the bells is among people, even alone in a room.
- Memory holds to places more than to times. A place does not change its light or its cold, so a person who goes back to where a thing happened finds it as it was.
- "Forward's the only direction that makes sense". People do not make much of what they have reached. They ask whether a person is still going.

## People

- **Children are few and public.** A birth needs an authorization, so a child is the pride of a whole lane. A child is never alone and never out of sight, and when it grows it must learn to want that.
- **The old remember the embargo.** The young have heard of it and are tired of hearing. This is the usual line of a quarrel in a family.
- **The young look along the belt.** Stability bores them, and the Far Edge is the place of their talk.
- **Earth is a word.** Nobody is homesick for it.
- **Those with an ability** are honored, and people are careful near them. Their neighbors watch them for the first signs of harm, and they know that they are watched.

## A Good Day

The grain is heading. The cistern is full. Nobody at the table is missing. The roof hums. There is green leaf with the sima, and someone tells the old story badly, and everyone corrects them.

| **Tanga-Moto**, the Fire Land | Grey, with steam in it | Warm ground; damp near the springs | Sulfur, hot iron, wet rock | Vents, hammers | Baths; food cooked on vents; Deepkin faces in the markets |

## What Changes on a Journey

- **Dayward:** the sun stands higher, the air is warmer and drier, shadows shorten, and the grit begins.
- **Nightward:** the sun sinks, the air is colder and wetter, the ground rises, and the ground itself begins to glow.
- **Up-belt:** colder, windier, more rain, more words from the crew, thicker walls.
- **Down-belt:** drier, dustier, less water in the cup, longer greetings.
- Along the belt the sun stays at the same height for the whole journey. Across the belt it changes with each day of walking. A traveler who has crossed the belt has seen the sun move, and few other people ever do.

# Under the Night

The life of the Deepkin (*pango-jata*), from the inside.

- **Light.** It comes from living things: the blue-green of glowcaps, murals that grow, markers on the walls. It comes from the floor and the walls as often as from above, so a face is lit from beneath. Nothing has a fixed shadow. Near a vent the rock itself is a dim red.
- **Air.** Warm, damp and still. Hair lies flat. A flame stands straight.
- **Smell.** Mineral water, fungus, a little sulfur; steam from the kitchens; warm stone; many people in one place. There is no smoke: food is steamed or cooked on the vents.
- **Sound.** Every sound goes far and comes back. People speak low and slowly, and they hold their long vowels. A person is known by their step. Stone bells carry signals and chimes ring in the warm air that rises. For some work nobody speaks at all, and fingers with glowing marks do the talking. In the outer tunnels the silence is complete.
- **Warmth is the measure of a place.** Near the Hearth it is warm. In the outer caverns it is cold. Families change rooms by rota so that nobody keeps the cold for all time. People own little, and they belong to the Hearth more than to a room.
- **Time.** The caves keep the same count of ten bells, by stone bell and chime, because trade needs one count.
- **Water** is warm. It comes from steam. People sit in the pools and are cleaner than any Belter, and they are as sparing with every other thing.
- **Clothes** are light and carry dyes that glow.
- **The table:** pale fungi with a taste of nuts, blind fish, algae, the shellfish of the hot pools, young fronds of the geotherm fern, buns.
- **Company.** Glowmoss grazers are kept near the houses. A guest is received with care and trusted late.
- **The shape of a community.** The vent and the Warmth Hearth are at the bottom, with the working caverns beside them. The rooms lie above in rings that grow colder outward. The cold vault is near the ice, and the Deep Road comes in from the side. Heat rises from the vent in three main channels, and a central shaft carries the chimes. The hot pools take their water from the steam condensers. The bone wall is one wall of the Hearth; the living murals are on the other.
- **Catching sickness.** There is no dayward side below, so a sick person lies at the cold end, in the outermost ring, where the air leaves the community toward the cold. The cave weatherworkers keep the draught going outward past the sick.

# Crossing Over

**A Belter below.** For the first days the body waits for the storm, because the air is still. There is no day side, so there is no direction, and some people are sick with it. The silence stops their sleep. The warmth feels heavy. Their red cloth looks black in the green light, and their voice is too loud for every room. Then the ears open, and the warm water does the rest.

**A Deepkin above.** The sky has no roof. Many keep their eyes on the ground and walk close to walls. The wind is a hand on the body that never lets go, and one side of the face is always cold. The red light is hard on eyes that are made for the dark, and they cover them. The cloth that glowed at home is dull. Nothing is ever quiet, and the hum of the roof stops their sleep. They find the people loud, dusty and careless of what they have.

Each side looks dull and dark in the light of the other.

# At the Gate

A trade day at a gate of the Deep Roads, as at Motowan.

- **The mouth.** The air of the tunnel is warm and damp, and the wind of the belt is cooler and drier. Where they meet there is mist. From far off, the mouth of a Deep Road is a white plume that leans dayward.
- **Smell.** Mineral water, fungus and sulfur come up from below and meet dust and lamp oil. A Belter smells the caves before the first Deepkin comes into view.
- **Two lamps.** The gate hall is dim, because the red light is hard on eyes that are made for the dark. Glowcap jars stand on the cave side and oil lamps on the belt side. A color is not the same under each lamp, so a buyer carries cloth or dye across the hall before anybody says a price. "Show it under both lamps" is also what people say when they doubt a story.
- **Sound.** The Deepkin speak low and slowly, and the Belters are too loud for them. Much of the trade is by hand sign. The Deepkin add the glowing marks on their fingers. A deal closes on *nzuri*.
- **The bells.** Both sides keep the same ten bells. The stone bell in the tunnel answers the bell house of the city, one stroke late.
- **What comes up:** ice packed in straw, minerals, dried fungi and blind fish, glowing dyes, geothermal crystals in double crates.
- **What goes down:** grain, cloth, tools and forged parts.
- **The judge.** A water judge tests each load of ice before it goes on and reads the tokens that come with it.
- **The cup.** Each side gives the cup to the other before trade begins. The Deepkin receive a guest with care and trust late: a trader who has come to the same gate for twenty Cycles still hears the same polite questions.
- **The envoy's table.** A Wind and Flame Envoy sits between the two sides. When a dispute is open, the thing in dispute lies on that table, as it would lie in the circle of a council. Trade goes on around it.
- **How it feels.** Each side finds the other a little rude and does not say so. The Belter thinks that the Deepkin answer too slowly. The Deepkin think that the Belter wastes words and brings dust. The children of both sides stare. After the last bell each side goes home pleased with the price, and sure that the other one was difficult.
